Hear Them Out teaches Cozy’s front-desk staff to handle a complaint call, then follow up with a clear process so no complaint gets missed. The case study found three root causes behind members’ unhappiness, and the course serves the first one: no follow-up process or call practice for complaints. Cozy’s managers change the log, with the follow-up date and the owner required, a reminder and a check at each shift change, and the course teaches the call and the follow-up. This document sets out how the course works and why. The storyboard holds every line.

The learner takes an upset member’s call, then logs the case and follows it up.

The course is a branching scenario in two parts, each 15 minutes or less. The learner is on the front desk when Sofia, a member, calls, upset: yesterday another member took a half-hour call next to her in Focus, the silent zone. Part 1 is that call, with five decisions, one for each step of HEARD, a service recovery technique taught by the Disney Institute: Hear, Empathize, Apologize, Resolve and Diagnose. Sofia speaks her replies, and her mood changes with each one. If a decision went wrong, Sofia scores Cozy in the member survey a week later, a debrief marks each decision, and the learner rewinds. If all five went right, the learner moves on to Part 2. The learner keeps a note of the call, and in Part 2 logs the case from it in Cozy Desk, a practice copy of the front-desk software. The course then moves ahead in time to the call back on the promised day, and Sofia scores Cozy 5/5.

Son, a guide, stays with the learner throughout. After each decision, he describes Sofia’s mood, because on the phone the learner cannot see her face. His lines are text, one at a time.

One message runs through the course: a member who complains needs to feel heard, and needs a solution delivered on the day promised.

The case. Sofia has been a member for eight months and works at the window desk in Focus. Two weeks ago, she reported calls in Focus, and nobody replied. Three facts drive the call:

  • What she needs: quiet mornings she can count on for focused work.
  • Who made the call: Dan, a new member, who thought quiet calls were fine in Focus.
  • What Cozy got wrong: her report had no owner or follow-up, so nobody spoke to Dan, and it happened again.

Other issues go elsewhere: Wi-Fi outages to the Wi-Fi Down card, money questions to the head of operations, and safety to the incident process.

This course is built to raise the complaint score from 2.7/5 to 3.8/5 within two quarters.

That is part of the board’s goal of raising overall satisfaction from 3.2/5 to 4.0/5. The course uses the complaint score because a better call changes it directly.

The course is built around Jane, a front-desk staff member who studies in the quiet gaps of a shift.

Front-desk staff handle complaints between tours, events and plan changes, and study for 20 to 30 minutes per shift when it is quiet, on the desk computer or their phone. Jane is a persona based on the shift interviews and the issue log. She has three years at Cozy and knows most members by name.

With action mapping, I chose five actions that resolve a complaint.

Each action leads to the goal and has a practice and the minimum information it needs (Moore, 2017). The case study sorted each cause into the six cells of the Behavior Engineering Model (BEM), and the action map keeps the same split. Barriers in knowledge and skill, such as explaining the rule first or ending with “we’ll look into it”, go to the course. Barriers in the environment, marked → Ops, go to Cozy’s managers.

One terminal objective covers the whole task, and five enabling objectives build it, one for each step of HEARD.

The terminal objective is TO, and the enabling objectives are EO1 to EO5. The verbs are at Bloom’s apply, analyze and evaluate levels, because the learner uses rules and judges options in a realistic case.

  • TO: During a practice phone call from a member who is upset about another member’s call in Focus, the staff member will apply HEARD to handle the call, keep a note of it, and then log it in Cozy Desk from that note, reaching the best survey answer, 5/5, and saving a log entry with every scored field right.
  • EO1 (Hear): Given an upset member’s opening on a call, the staff member will choose the reply that lets the member tell the whole story, before any closed question or rule, and pick the log entry that says what happened and what the member needs.
  • EO2 (Empathize): Given the member’s account, the staff member will choose the reply that names her feeling and says her need back, before any rule or solution.
  • EO3 (Apologize): Given a member whose earlier note got no follow-up and who asks who made the call, the staff member will choose the apology that says what Cozy got wrong and keeps names private on both sides.
  • EO4 (Resolve): Given the member’s need and Cozy’s options, the staff member will select the option that fits the need, with an owner and a day, and enter the next step, the owner and a dated to-do for each promise in the log.
  • EO5 (Diagnose): Given a case at the end of the call, the staff member will determine its status, keeping it open until the member says the solution worked, and identify it in the log by member, contact date, issue type and zone.

Each item is marked Right or Not yet on its first try. Passing asks for mastery: the path to 5/5 and a log saved with every scored field right. The tapped lines and the note are not scored, and writing the log entries in the learner’s own words is practiced after the course.

The course is one phone call and one log, with short screens around them.

The call.

  • One thing at a time. Sofia speaks first, then Son, then the learner moves. After decision 1, Son’s reading of her mood and his next line come as one message, with no Next, and the line to say comes in with it.
  • The note. Any line of the call can go into it. Its first line, who is calling and when, goes in before the learner answers. At decision 1, Son points to Sofia’s first line and asks the learner to note it. There are two ways on: his Next, or noting that line, whose Note button is lit while his line waits.
  • Hanging up. The first press of End drops the line, or the learner calls Sofia back and says sorry. The second ends the call: Sofia gives 1/5, and the call starts again.
  • Real time. The call runs in real time, and the promised days are real working days.

Sofia’s survey answer. A call with the right solution but a cold manner gets 3/5, more than a kind call with no solution, which gets 2/5: once the mornings are quiet, a cold call fades, but the noise comes back every morning.

Part 2. Cozy Desk works for any case, like the real software, with twelve issue types and a to-do list with a day for each promise. The desk computer’s sign-in and welcome sit on a wallpaper of Cozy’s floor plan, with the lamp’s glow. The first time, Son tours it one part at a time. The learner logs the case from the note alone, and when a field is wrong, Son shows which key lines the note was missing. The fast forward then shows the handover at shift change, the talk with Dan and the call back.

The course measures Kirkpatrick’s levels 1 and 2, and the issue log and the member survey measure levels 3 and 4.

In Thalheimer’s LTEM, the decisions are tier 5, a decision in a realistic case, and the log is tier 6, a task done in a practice tool. Results are saved in the browser. In a rollout, an LMS would record each item with its HEARD step, the first-try score, the pass and the two answers.

The first-try results show which step of HEARD is hardest, so any change to the course starts there.

Every main choice in the design has a reason behind it.

Three job aids hold the minimum information from the action map: the HEARD card, the Options card and the log card.

The first two open after the first ending, and the log card joins them in Part 2. Each can be printed from the course.

The log entry for Sofia’s case, as the course checks it:

The talk is right on any day before [day 2], and the call back only on [day 2]. The case stays open until Sofia says the solution worked.

After the course, the learner can practice more if they want, in three ways: a new call, a private talk and a log entry to write.

The private talk is with Dan, in five multiple-choice questions, not scored, with feedback after each choice. The right choices show the approach: talk privately, keep the reporter’s name out, give a friendly tip that names the place and the alternative, let Dan keep face, and still have the talk.

Q1. When and where do you talk to Dan?

Q2. Dan asks: “Who complained?”

Q3. How do you describe the problem?

Q4. Dan says: “Sorry, I thought quiet calls were fine in Focus.”

Q5. The talk went well. What do you do next?
