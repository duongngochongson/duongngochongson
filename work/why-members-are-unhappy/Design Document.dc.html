Over one year, overall member satisfaction at Cozy fell from 4.1 to 3.2 out of 5. Members give Cozy 2.7 out of 5 for how it handles complaints, the lowest score in the member survey. When an upset member calls, front-desk staff often explain the rules before they ask what happened. They say sorry in general terms and end with "we'll look into it", with no options and no date. About half of the complaints in the issue log, where staff record complaints, have a written next step, and some members have waited weeks for a reply. The needs analysis, which looked for the causes of the drop before choosing any fix, found two parts to this problem. The log does not ask staff for a follow-up date or an owner, so Cozy's managers change the log. Staff have also not practiced calming an upset member and agreeing on a fix, so this course teaches the call and the follow-up.

This document describes how the course works and the reason for each choice. It covers the scenario, the goal, the learner, the action map, the objectives and how the scored items match them, the interactions, the evaluation plan, the design decisions, the job aids, which are short reference cards that staff use on the job, and the exercise after the course. The storyboard, a separate document, holds every line and screen of the course.

The course is a branching scenario, in which each choice the learner makes changes what happens next. It has two parts, and each part takes 15 minutes or less, about 20 minutes in all. The learner plays a member of the front-desk staff on shift at Cozy. The phone rings, and Sofia, a member, is on the line. She is upset because another member took a long phone call next to her in Focus, the silent zone. Part 1 is that call. It plays on a phone screen in real time and has five decisions, one for each step of HEARD, a five-step way of handling complaints taught by the Disney Institute: Hear, Empathize, Apologize, Resolve and Diagnose. Between the decisions, the learner says a few set lines by tapping them. Sofia replies to each choice in her own voice, and her mood changes with each reply. The learner keeps their own note of the call as they go. In Part 2, the learner turns to the desk computer and logs the case from that note in Cozy Desk, a practice copy of the software Cozy's front desk uses. The course then moves ahead in time and follows the case through to the call back. After the course, two of the three practice activities add new cases: a call from another member about calls in Flex, and a log entry about labeled food thrown out at the fridge clean-up.

Son is a guide character who stays with the learner through the course, from the first screen to the last. He explains how things work and has the learner start the note before answering. After each decision, he describes Sofia's mood, because on a phone call the learner cannot see her face. Son has no voice, and his lines are text. Before the phone turns over, he writes in chat messages. From then on, his lines appear in a bar of his own at the top of the screen, apart from the call, one line at a time, each when the learner taps his button.

The course has one message, which it states early and shows often: a member who complains needs to feel heard, and needs a fix they can count on, delivered on the day promised. A quick fix without listening leaves the member unhappy, and Sofia's survey answers in the course show this.

The practice case. Sofia has a hot desk plan, which lets her use any free desk in Focus or Flex. She has been a member for eight months and usually works at the window desk in Focus. Today she calls from home: "I'm working from home today so I can actually get something done." Yesterday morning, while she was preparing a client presentation, another member took a half-hour call next to her. Two weeks ago she left a note at the front desk about calls in Focus. It went into the log with no owner and no follow-up, so nobody replied and nobody spoke to the member who made the calls. Now she wants to know who made the call.

The case rests on three facts.

  • What the member needs: quiet mornings they can count on for focused work.
  • The member who made the call: Dan, a new member, who thought quiet calls were fine in Focus.
  • What Cozy got wrong: her first report had no owner or follow-up, so nobody spoke to Dan, and it happened again. Part 2 practices the fix for this: every case gets an owner, and every promise gets a day.

The two parts of the course cover the call and the work that follows it.

  • Part 1: the call. The learner practices the five steps of HEARD. Hear, Empathize, Apologize and Resolve come during the call, and Diagnose comes right after it, when the learner sets the status of the case.
  • Part 2: after the call. The learner uses the new follow-up process that Cozy's managers set up. They log the case with an owner and a day for every promise, so the list of open cases hands it over to the next shift, and they call the member back on that day.

The course leaves other issues to other people and tools. Wi-Fi outages follow the Wi-Fi Down card, a checklist kept at the front desk. Money questions (discounts, refunds, billing) go to the head of operations, who manages the buildings, the internet and the suppliers. Safety problems and harassment follow the incident process, Cozy's separate procedure for serious incidents. The private talk with the member who made the call is a short exercise after the course (section 11).

The course goal is to raise members' rating of how Cozy handles their complaints from 2.7 to 3.8 out of 5 within the next two quarters. It is part of Cozy's business goal for the same period, which is to raise overall member satisfaction from 3.2 to 4.0, with 3.5 after the first quarter. Other fixes work toward the business goal as well. Cozy's managers change the issue log. Cozy in 60 seconds is a short guide for new members with the zone map, the house rules and the signs they will see in the space. The Wi-Fi Down card is a checklist for staff during an outage.

The course goal uses the complaint score because a better call changes that score directly. Overall satisfaction, and whether a member stays or leaves, depend on more than one call. In the table, a leading indicator is an early measure that moves before the survey does, and a guardrail is a rule that must never be broken on the way to the goal.

The learners are Cozy's front-desk staff. They work at the front desks in shifts. Complaints come in between welcome tours, member events, questions and plan changes. They can study for 20 to 30 minutes per shift when it is quiet, on the front-desk computer or their phone.

The design follows Jane, a persona. A persona is a made-up but typical learner, and Jane is based on the shift interviews and the issue log from the needs analysis.

Jane's profile

  • Experience: She has worked at Cozy for three years and knows most members by name.
  • Work: Her day shifts are busy with tours, events and plan changes, so she wants calls to be short.
  • Learning: She studies on the front-desk computer or her phone in the quiet hour after lunch. A call or a member at the desk can interrupt her.
  • Common mistakes: She explains the Focus rule before asking what happened. She names the member who made the call, because she knows everyone: "That must be Dan, he's new." She offers a quick fix that does not match the need, like earplugs or a free coffee. She hands over open cases by word of mouth.
  • What stops her learning: She stops when the course tells her what she already knows, or when she loses her place after an interruption.

The course content comes from action mapping, a method for planning training created by Cathy Moore. An action map starts from the business goal and lists what people need to do to reach it. Each action gets a practice activity and the minimum information staff need for it, meaning the facts the practice calls for and nothing more. For each action, the map also lists the barriers, meaning what keeps staff from doing it. Five actions lead to the goal: three during the call (Part 1) and two after it (Part 2). They follow the steps of HEARD and the research on complaint handling listed in the case study. Moore sorts barriers into four types: environment, knowledge, skill and motivation. The barriers found here are of three types: environment, knowledge and skill. The course deals with the knowledge and skill barriers. Environment barriers need a change to the process, so they go to Cozy's managers. This follows the Six Boxes, Carl Binder's version of Thomas Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model, which checks the environment first because changing it usually costs less than training. In the figure and the table, these barriers are marked "→ Ops", short for operations, and the changes for managers are listed below the table.

The call brings the actions together. It has five decisions, one for each step of HEARD. Decisions 1 to 3 make up the talk axis, which is about how the call felt to Sofia, and decisions 4 and 5 make up the resolve axis, which is about whether her problem was solved. Together, the two axes give one of four survey answers. The learner's note carries the call into the log, and Part 2 follows the case through to the call back. After the course, the learner can try three practice activities. In the practice call, an AI plays the member, and a second AI checks the call against the criteria. The private talk with Dan is a short exercise of five multiple-choice questions. In the written practice, the learner types two log entries, and an AI checks them against the same criteria as the course. Neither the practice call nor the written practice is scored, because AI scoring can vary.

Changes for managers (→ Ops). Cozy's managers make these changes in the first quarter. The course teaches the new process, so the changes and the course start at the same time.

The objectives say what the learner will be able to do at the end of the course. The terminal objective (TO) is the overall one, and it covers the whole task: the call, the note and the log. Five enabling objectives (EO1 to EO5) are the smaller skills that build it, one for each step of HEARD, so each scored item belongs to one step. Each objective uses a verb from the revised Bloom's taxonomy, a common list of six levels of thinking: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate and create. The verbs here are at the apply, analyze and evaluate levels, because the learner uses rules and judges options in a realistic case.

  • TO: During a practice phone call from a member who is upset about another member's call in Focus, the staff member will apply HEARD to handle the call, keep a note of it, and then log it in Cozy Desk from that note, reaching the best survey answer, 5 out of 5, and saving a log entry with every scored field right.
  • EO1 (Hear): Given an upset member's opening on a call, the staff member will choose the reply that lets the member tell the whole story, before any closed question (one that asks for a yes or a no) or any rule, and pick the log entry that says what happened and what the member needs.
  • EO2 (Empathize): Given the member's account, the staff member will choose the reply that names her feeling and says her need back, before any rule or fix.
  • EO3 (Apologize): Given a member whose earlier note got no follow-up and who asks who made the call, the staff member will choose the apology that says what Cozy got wrong and keeps names private on both sides.
  • EO4 (Resolve): Given the member's need and Cozy's options, the staff member will select the option that fits the need, with an owner and a day, and enter the next step, the owner and a dated to-do for each promise in the log.
  • EO5 (Diagnose): Given a case at the end of the call, the staff member will determine its status, keeping it open until the member says the fix worked, and identify it in the log by member, contact date, issue type and zone, so the managers' weekly review can find the cause.

Alignment means that each objective is practiced and scored in a way that matches it. Each objective has scored items in the call or the log, and each item also gives practice and feedback. Robert Gagné sorted what people learn into kinds of capability, such as applying rules, using concepts and solving problems, and he argued that each kind is learned under its own conditions. Here, rules are applied in decisions with consequences, and the procedure is done in a practice tool, Cozy Desk, from the learner's own note. Each scored item is marked Right or Not yet on its first try, and the results screen shows how many items were right on the first try. Passing is a separate measure that asks for mastery, meaning every item right in the end: the best path through the call, and a log saved with every scored field right. The learner gets there through the rewind and the log's checks.

The lines the learner taps to say, the to-do tasks they tap to fill in, and the note itself are not scored. In the course, the learner fills in the two text fields of the log by picking an entry, from two for each field: one meets both of the field's criteria, and the other is wrong. Writing the entries is practiced in the written practice after the course, where the grader checks the learner's own words against the same criteria.

The course is built around one phone call and one log, with short screens before, between and after them. The storyboard holds the full text, the options and the feedback.

  1. 1

On any other path, the course jumps ahead a week and shows Sofia's answer in the member survey. The debrief follows, and then a rewind to the first decision that went wrong.
  5. 5

At shift change, the case is on the list of open cases. On [day 1], the learner talks with the member who made the call, and on [day 2], the learner calls Sofia back as promised and closes the case.     After Part 2. A week later, Sofia gives 5 out of 5 in the member survey.

    Results and practice. The results screen shows how many items were right on the first try and which steps of HEARD to practice. It asks two questions, and Son answers with a next step. From there, the learner can print the job aids, try the three practice activities or restart the course.

The call. The call screen works as follows.

  • Layout: On a computer, the phone sits on the left and the conversation on the right, with Son's bar at the top right, under the "Pause and save" button. On a phone, a call bar sits at the top of the screen, with Son's bar under it.
  • Order: Sofia's lines come first, then Son's, then the learner's move, so one thing at a time asks for attention. Each of Son's lines waits until the learner taps his button. His last line before a move stays open while the move, such as the choices, comes in with it.
  • The note: It is one list in the order the lines were taken, like a paper note. Any line in the call, Sofia's or the learner's, can go into it. Its first line is "Sofia · [call date] · [call time]", with the date and time at which the learner starts the note. It goes in while the incoming call is still quiet, and noting it makes the phone ring.
  • End: The End button works all through the call. What happens the first time the learner presses it depends on when. Before the greeting, the line drops and Sofia calls again. Later in the call, Sofia is cut off, and the learner calls her back and says sorry. The second time, the call is over with an ending of 1 out of 5, and the learner can start the call again. At the goodbye, pressing End is the expected move.
  • Time: The call runs in real time. The call timer starts at Accept and stops while the course is paused. The dates in the lines are worked out from the day of the call, in working days, and saved with the progress. When a weekend comes between [day 1] and [day 2], decision 4's promise names the next working day, for example "call you Monday, the next working day after that". After a rewind, the call timer goes on from where it was and does not go back.

The branch map. The figure and the table show each beat of the call, meaning each moment in order. For each decision, they give the best option, the weaker options and the axis the decision counts toward.

Sofia's survey answer. A week after the call, Sofia answers the member survey. Talk is right when decisions 1 to 3 all take the best option, and resolve is right when decisions 4 and 5 both do. The two axes give four possible answers, and an answer below 5 shows which part of the call went wrong, or that both did.

A call with the right fix but a cold manner gets 3 out of 5, more than a kind call with no fix, which gets 2. The survey comes a week after the call: once the mornings are quiet, a call that felt cold fades, but the noise in a call with no fix comes back every morning. The note under the figure covers two more cases: hanging up twice, and sharing Dan's name.

Part 2. The log in Cozy Desk and the scenes after it work as follows.

  • Cozy Desk handles any case. Like the software at Cozy's front desk, it has twelve issue types, and the list of areas changes with the issue type, with more than one area allowed. The learner picks the dates and the owner. The owner list has five first names, the learner's and four teammates'. Each date field has four quick picks, "Today", "Yesterday", "In 1 day" and "In 2 days", and the last two count working days. Every promise goes in a to-do list with its day.
  • A tour the first time: Son shows Cozy Desk one part at a time. The rest of the screen is dimmed, and the part he talks about is lit: the member search, the note, the "Contacted", "Issue type" and "Zone or area" fields, the two entries, "Owner", the to-do list and "Save entry". During the tour, the learner looks at each part without filling it in, and "Next" or a tap anywhere goes on. Both rows of the to-do list are there from the start, and Son names the two promises they are for and says how the quick picks count. "Skip the tour" ends the tour, and "Show me around again" replays it.
  • The two text fields ("What happened" and "Next step") are filled in by picking an entry. Each field offers two entries, one right and one wrong. Writing them in the learner's own words is the written practice after the course.
  • The first save: The learner fills in the log from their own note, with nothing else to go on. When a field is wrong, Son shows which key lines the note was missing and reminds the learner to note what they are told and what they promise.
  • The fast forward: At shift change, the list of open cases shows Sofia's case with its owner and to-dos, and Son says the evening shift has everything it needs. On [day 1], the learner has the talk with the member who made the call. On [day 2], a reminder comes up, and the learner calls Sofia back. She says the fix worked, and the case status then has one option, "Close the case".

The screens. The table lists each screen with its interaction, the objective it serves and its feedback. Consequence feedback shows what happens because of a choice, such as Sofia's reply. Corrective feedback says what was right or wrong and why.

The plan uses two common ways of describing what an evaluation measures. Donald Kirkpatrick's four levels ask how learners react to the training (level 1), what they learn (level 2), whether they change what they do at work (level 3) and whether the business results improve (level 4). The course itself measures levels 1 and 2, and levels 3 and 4 use the issue log and the member survey after launch. Will Thalheimer's Learning-Transfer Evaluation Model (LTEM) sorts measures into eight tiers, from attendance at tier 1 to the effects of applying the learning at work at tier 8. The higher the tier, the closer the measure comes to real work. Here, the five decisions are a tier 5 measure, a decision made in a realistic case. The log is a tier 6 measure, a realistic task done in a practice tool, with its fields checked by fixed rules. The practice call and the written practice also work at tier 6, in the learner's own words, but they are not scored. The two questions at the end show how learners see their own readiness, which is tier 3.

The course saves the results and the learner's answers in the browser, on the learner's own device. In a rollout to Cozy's staff, the same data would go to a learning management system (LMS), the software companies use to give courses to staff and record the results. The LMS would record each scored item with its HEARD step, the first-try score, the pass and the two answers.

The first-try results show which step of HEARD learners find hardest, so any later change to the course starts with that step.
