In one year, overall member satisfaction at Cozy fell from 4.1/5 to 3.2/5, and the score for how Cozy handles complaints became the lowest in the member survey, at 2.7/5. The analysis found three causes and chose a solution for each one: signs in the space and a first-day guide for members, a Wi-Fi Down checklist for staff, and, for complaints, changes to the issue log with Hear Them Out, a branching course for front-desk staff. The goal is 4.0/5 overall and 3.8/5 for complaints within two quarters.

Cozy rents desks and rooms in three coworking spaces to freelancers, remote workers and small teams from many countries. Each space has three zones: Focus is silent, Flex is for desk work, calls and meetings, and Private has closed rooms. Front-desk staff, whom Cozy calls community managers, welcome members, handle complaints and run events in shifts, with 20 to 30 minutes for learning in quiet periods.

In the quarterly member survey, members name three problems: noise and calls in Focus, the shared kitchen, and Wi-Fi outages. The issue log, where staff record complaints, shows a fourth: only about half of the complaints have a written next step, and some members waited weeks for a reply. Two groups have to act differently:

Members need to follow the space rules, such as no calls in Focus and a name and a date on food in the fridge. They see the rules once, in the handbook they get when they join.
Front-desk staff handle outages and complaints. Jane, the persona, is based on the shift interviews and the issue log. On a complaint call, she explains the Focus rule before asking what happened, offers a quick fix and hands open cases over by word of mouth.

Noise in Focus and the kitchen. Noise is the most common dislike in Deskmag's global survey of coworking members, for about 1 in 4 of them. At Cozy, members are unsure which zone they are in and when the fridge is cleared, and the visit showed why: Focus and Flex look the same, and the rules sit in the handbook. That is a gap in information. Members could follow the rules if they had to, so they need to see them where they work, not a course.

Wi-Fi outages. In Deskmag's first survey, 99% of members named internet access as the service they need most. At Cozy, each shift handles an outage in its own order, most wait for the cause before telling members, and each decides compensation on its own. Staff also switch to the backup line by hand. That is a gap in information and tools: staff can do every step, but outages are rare and stressful, so a checklist and an automatic switch fit better than a course.

Complaint handling. Public reviews of coworking spaces describe promises with no follow-up. At Cozy, the issue log asks for no follow-up date and no owner, and open cases pass between shifts by word of mouth, so following up depends on memory. That part of the cause is in the environment. In the shift interviews, staff answered one question:

"A member calls, upset about noise. What do you say?"

Shift interviews · the question

They explained the rules before asking what happened, apologized in general terms and ended with "we'll look into it". Staff could write a date if the log asked for one, but calming an upset member and agreeing on a solution take practice, so that part needs a course.

Choosing the kind of solution. Two causes are in the environment, so information and tools solve them sooner and for less: Chapman's benchmarks put a half-hour branching scenario at 90 to 360 hours of work, against a few days for a checklist and signs. The third cause needs both a change to the log and a course, which start in the same quarter.

Signs and Cozy in 60 seconds, for members

Prompts work best where people act: at recycling bins, specific prompts raised correct recycling by 54%, against 17% for the same prompts 4 meters away (Austin et al., 1993). So a sign marks where Focus starts, colored tape marks the edge of each zone, and labels in the kitchen show how the fridge and the shelves work. Each one says what to do and what to do instead, with one icon, word and color for each zone. Cozy in 60 seconds, eight short screens in the Cozy app, shows the zones and the signs on a member's first day.

Wi-Fi Down card, for front-desk staff and operations

A one-page checklist at the front desk gives every shift the same seven steps: check the scope, tell everyone within 10 minutes, get the Wi-Fi back, send an update every 15 minutes, call the head of operations at 30 minutes, thank everyone and log the outage. A wait with no known end feels longer, so the first notice goes out before the cause is known (Maister, 1985). Everyone present gets the same compensation on every shift, because a procedure feels fair when it is applied the same way (Leventhal, 1980). The head of operations also makes the switch to the backup line automatic.

Log changes and Hear Them Out, for managers and front-desk staff

The log changes. Cozy's managers make the follow-up date and the owner required in the log, add a reminder on that date and a check of open cases at each shift change, and confirm which options staff may offer. Every week, they check the log for missed follow-ups, and the same check tracks the 9 in 10 target. Structured logs based on checklists prevent failures at handover (ASM Consortium, 2011).

The course. Hear Them Out teaches front-desk staff to handle a complaint call and to follow the case through, in about 20 minutes on a computer or a phone. Action mapping set its five actions, from hearing the member out to calling back on the promised day, and sent barriers in the work to the managers instead. Sofia, a member, calls because another member took a half-hour call next to her in Focus, two weeks after she reported calls there. Son, a guide, describes her mood after each reply, because on the phone the learner cannot see her face.

  • One call (Part 1). Five decisions, one for each step of HEARD: Hear, Empathize, Apologize, Resolve and Diagnose. Each weaker option holds one mistake, and several are the habits the shift interviews found.
  • Endings the learner can see. A week later, Sofia scores Cozy in the member survey: 5/5, 3/5, 2/5 or 1/5, depending on how the call felt (decisions 1 to 3) and whether her problem was solved (4 and 5). A debrief marks each decision, and the learner rewinds to the first one that went wrong.
  • From the note to the log (Part 2). After a call that went right, the learner logs the case in Cozy Desk from their own call note, then follows it through shift change to the call back.
  • Less support as skill grows. After the course, the learner writes their own words: a practice call with Omar, a member played by an AI model and checked by a second one, and a log entry. Three job aids, the HEARD, Options and log cards, hold what the learner needs to know.

The action map, the branch map of the call and the reason for each design choice are in the design document.

The storyboard holds every line of the course, and the style guide sets its look. The course is a web app built with React, XState, Zod and Motion, and hosted on Vercel. A test checks every line of the course against the storyboard, word for word. Progress is saved after every step, so a learner who is interrupted on shift comes back to the same place.

If the log reaches 9 in 10 but the complaint score stays at 3.2/5, staff are following up on time, so the next question is whether the options they offer meet members' needs.
