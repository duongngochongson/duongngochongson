In one year, overall member satisfaction at Cozy fell from 4.1 to 3.2 out of 5. The score for how Cozy handles complaints became the lowest in the member survey, at 2.7. The analysis traced the drop to three causes and chose a fix for each one. Members saw the space rules once, in a handbook, so the first fix puts the rules where members work: signs in the space, and Cozy in 60 seconds, a short guide for a new member's first day. Each shift handled Wi-Fi outages in its own way, so the second fix is a one-page checklist that sets one order of steps and one rule for compensating members, along with an automatic switch to the backup internet line. Complaint handling needed two changes. Cozy's managers make the follow-up date and the owner required fields in the log. Front-desk staff, whom Cozy calls community managers, take Hear Them Out, a course that teaches them to handle a complaint call and to follow the case through. The course is a branching scenario, in which each choice the learner makes changes what happens next. The goal is to raise overall satisfaction to 4.0 and the complaint score to 3.8 within two quarters.

Cozy is a coworking business: it rents desks and rooms in three shared workspaces. Its members are freelancers, remote workers and small startup teams from many countries. Each space has three work zones. Focus is silent, Flex is for desk work, calls and meetings, and Private has closed rooms. Each space also has meeting rooms and a shared kitchen. Members pay for one of two monthly plans. A hot desk plan gives any free desk in Focus or Flex, and a room in Private by the hour. A dedicated plan gives a room of one's own in Private, plus any free desk in Focus or Flex.

Front-desk staff work at the front desk of each space in shifts. They welcome new members, answer questions, handle complaints, run events and change members' plans. In quiet periods, they can spend 20 to 30 minutes of a shift on learning, at the front-desk computer or on their phone. A head of operations manages the buildings, the internet (a main line and a backup line at each space) and the suppliers.

Every quarter, Cozy runs a member survey with scores from 1 to 5. In their survey comments, members name three problems: noise and phone calls in Focus, the shared kitchen and fridge, and Wi-Fi outages. The survey scores and the issue log, where staff record complaints, point to a fourth problem, the way complaints are handled. About half of the complaints in the log have a written next step, meaning what Cozy will do about it, and several members waited weeks for a reply.

Members need to follow the space rules: no calls in Focus, and a name and a date on food in the fridge. They see the rules once, in the handbook they get when they join, and they bring different habits about noise and sharing from many cultures.
Front-desk staff handle outages and complaints. To design for them, I used a persona called Jane. A persona is a made-up but typical learner, and Jane is based on the shift interviews and the issue log. She has three years at Cozy, knows most members by name and wants calls short. She studies in the quiet hour after lunch and is often interrupted. On a complaint call, she explains the Focus rule before asking what happened, offers a quick fix such as earplugs or a free coffee, and hands open cases over by word of mouth.

Noise in Focus and the shared kitchen. Deskmag, an online magazine about coworking, runs a global survey of coworking members. In its survey, noise is the most common dislike: about 1 in 4 members are annoyed by it. Those members rate their space a little lower than others do, 8.2 against 8.5 out of 10. At Cozy, survey comments show members unsure which zone they are in, what its rules are and when the fridge is cleared. The visit to one of the spaces showed one reason: Focus and Flex look the same. The rules are written in the handbook, which members read once, when they join, and nothing in the space shows them. In the Six Boxes, that is a gap in information. Members could follow the rules if they had to, so a course would not help. They need to see the rules where they work.

Wi-Fi outages. In Deskmag's first survey, 99% of members named internet access as the service they need most. At Cozy, the shift interviews and the outage log show each shift taking the steps in its own order: some switch to the backup line first, and some call the internet provider first. Most shifts wait until they know the cause before telling members, and each shift decides on its own what to give members to make up for an outage. New staff learn by shadowing a colleague for two days, so each shift keeps its own habits. Staff also switch to the backup line by hand. In the Six Boxes, that is a gap in information and tools. Staff can do every step if they have to, but outages are rare and stressful, so steps get done in the wrong order. A checklist at the front desk gives them the steps in order, and an automatic switch to the backup line removes one of the steps.

Complaint handling. Public reviews of coworking spaces describe promises with no follow-up and requests to the front desk that got no reply. In Deskmag's survey, liking the people who run the space raises members' ratings more than any other factor except liking the other members. At Cozy, the issue log does not ask for a follow-up date or for an owner, the person responsible for the case. Cases that are still open pass to the next shift by word of mouth. So following a case through depends on memory, and managers cannot see whether it happens. That part of the cause is in the environment: the log, the handover and what managers can see. In the shift interviews, staff also answered a question about one situation:

"A member calls, upset about noise. What do you say?"

Shift interviews · the question

Staff explained the rules before asking what happened, apologized in general terms and ended with "we'll look into it". That part of the cause is knowledge: staff have not learned how to handle an upset member. Staff could write a date if they had to, so the log should ask for it. Calming an upset member, admitting what Cozy got wrong and agreeing on a fix that meets the member's need take practice, so that part needs a course.

Choosing the kind of fix. Two of the three causes are in the environment, so information and tools fix them, sooner and at a lower cost than a course. Chapman's benchmarks estimate how many hours it takes to build training: they put a branching scenario of about half an hour at roughly 90 to 360 hours of development work, while a checklist and a set of signs take a few days. The third cause needs a change to the log and a course. Cozy's managers change the log, and the course gives front-desk staff practice in handling an upset member. The log changes and the course start in the same quarter.

Signs and Cozy in 60 seconds, for members

The rules go where members act. In one study, specific prompts placed at the recycling bins raised correct recycling by 54%, against 17% for the same prompts placed 4 meters away (Austin et al., 1993). So the signs go up in the space:

  • a Focus sign where Focus starts: "Silent zone. Calls and meetings in Flex", with an arrow in Flex's color
  • the zone map in colored tape along the zone edges, and on cards on the desks at the edge of Focus
  • in the kitchen: fridge labels with a name and a date, a "Help yourself" shelf, the Friday 6 pm clear-out, a sign for cups, and a message for leftover event food

Cozy in 60 seconds shows all of this on a new member's first day. It is a guide of eight short screens in the Cozy app, made for a phone. It shows the zone map, where to take a call, how the kitchen works and the signs the member will see in the space, so they recognize the signs later. It starts by asking how the member works, and the zone map then marks the zone that suits them: Focus for deep work, or Flex for calls.

The signs and the guide follow four rules. Each piece is placed where the behavior happens. It says what to do, and next to that what to do instead, such as "Calls and meetings in Flex". Every piece uses the same icon, word and color for each zone. And members see the rules from their first day. Focus stays silent, with no calls or meetings, so members get a quiet zone they can count on. In open-plan offices, noise that people cannot control and the loss of privacy are the main sources of dissatisfaction (Kim and de Dear, 2013).

The Wi-Fi Down card, for front-desk staff and operations

The Wi-Fi Down card is a job aid, a short reference card used on the job. It is a one-page checklist kept at the front desk, of the kind called READ-DO: during an outage, staff read each step and do it before going on to the next. Job aids suit tasks that are rare, complex or costly to get wrong (Rossett and Schafer, 2007). The card has seven steps:

  1. 1
    Within one minute, check the scope: is one device down, or the whole space?
  2. 2
    Send the first notice to members within 10 minutes, before the cause is known, and do not promise a time for the fix.
  3. 3
    Switch to the backup line. If the Wi-Fi is still down, restart the router, and then call the internet provider.
  4. 4
    Send an update every 15 minutes, including when nothing is new.
  5. 5
    If the Wi-Fi is still down after 30 minutes, call the head of operations.
  6. 6
    When the Wi-Fi is back, tell members and thank them. After an outage of 30 minutes or more, everyone present gets the same compensation.
  7. 7
    Log the outage: when it started, when the first notice went out, the cause, when it ended and the compensation.

Each step on the card names the mistake to avoid, and the card also holds the messages to send to members.

The timing comes from how waiting feels: a wait with no explanation and no known end feels longer than one with a promised update (Maister, 1985). Atlassian's guidance for teams that report outages of online services says to tell users early, update them at a set pace and take responsibility for the outage, including one caused by a supplier (Atlassian Statuspage). Compensation is the same for everyone present, on every shift, because people judge a procedure fair when it is applied consistently (Leventhal, 1980). Everyone present is offered a drink at the first notice. For an outage of 30 minutes or more, everyone present also gets one free hour in a room they can book within 30 days. This matches what members lost, which is working time (Roschk and Gelbrich, 2014). The analysis also asks the head of operations to make the switch to the backup line automatic, with an alert.

Log changes and Hear Them Out, for managers and front-desk staff

The log changes. In the first quarter, Cozy's managers make four changes:

  • make the follow-up date and the owner required fields in the log
  • add a handover step that goes through open cases at shift change
  • add a reminder on the follow-up date
  • confirm which options staff may offer an upset member, such as a desk in Focus or a private talk with another member

Every week, the managers check the log for missed follow-ups and cases left open, and the same check tracks the 9 in 10 target. In a study of hotels, front-line staff said that written instructions and support from colleagues help them put things right after a service failure (Schumacher and Komppula, 2016). Guidance on shift handover from the ASM Consortium, an industry research group, finds that structured logs based on checklists prevent failures at handover (ASM Consortium, 2011).

The course. Hear Them Out teaches front-desk staff to handle a complaint call and to follow the case through in the log. Each part takes 15 minutes or less, about 20 minutes in all, and the course works on the front-desk computer and on a phone. To decide what the course should teach, I used action mapping, a method for planning training created by Cathy Moore. An action map starts from the business goal and lists what people need to do to reach it. Each action gets a practice activity and the information staff need for it, which the map calls the minimum information. The map also lists barriers, meaning what keeps staff from doing an action. When a barrier is in the work itself, such as a log that does not ask for a date, Cozy's managers fix it instead of the course.

In the course, the learner is the front-desk staff member on shift. Sofia, a member, calls the front desk, upset. Yesterday another member took a half-hour phone call next to her in Focus, the silent zone. Two weeks before, she had reported calls in Focus, and her report went into the log with no owner and no follow-up. Son, a guide character who stays with the learner through the course, describes Sofia's mood after each of her replies, because on a phone call the learner cannot see her face. His lines are text, and he never gives a hint before a first try.

  • One call, in real time (Part 1). The call has five decisions, one for each step of HEARD, a five-step way of handling complaints taught by the Disney Institute: Hear, Empathize, Apologize, Resolve and Diagnose. Between the decisions, the learner taps the lines they say, such as the greeting. Each decision has a best option and weaker ones, and each weaker option contains one mistake, such as telling Sofia the other member's name or promising to act without saying when. Some of these mistakes are the habits the shift interviews found, such as explaining the rule before asking what happened. The best apology names what Cozy got wrong, because in a study of apologies, admitting responsibility was the part that helped an apology most (Lewicki, Polin and Lount, 2016). Names stay private on both sides, as ISO 10002, the international standard for handling complaints, asks. Sofia's replies are spoken and shown on screen, and her mood changes with each reply. The course starts with the call and leaves the rules for later, following Merrill's first principles of instruction, which say that learning should start from a real problem.
  • Endings the learner can see. Every path ends with Sofia's answer to the member survey question that the course goal is about, how Cozy handles complaints. Her score depends on two things, which the course calls the talk and resolve axes. Talk is whether the learner listened, showed empathy and apologized well in decisions 1 to 3. Resolve is whether decisions 4 and 5 solved her problem. That gives four possible scores, 5, 3, 2 or 1 out of 5, and a score below 5 shows which of the two parts went wrong, or that both did. This follows research showing that customers judge a complaint by the outcome, the process and how they were treated (Tax, Brown and Chandrashekaran, 1998). If any decision went wrong, the course jumps ahead a week once the call is over and shows Sofia's score. Then a debrief, a review of the call, marks each decision as right or not yet right. The learner can rewind to the first decision that went wrong, and the right choices before it are kept. This follows mastery learning, in which learners practice what they have not mastered yet and keep what they have.
  • From the note to the log (Part 2). Before answering the phone, the learner starts their own note of the call, and during the call they can add any line of the conversation to it. If all five decisions went right, Sofia hangs up and waits to see whether Cozy follows up, and Part 2 begins. The learner logs the case in Cozy Desk, a practice copy of the software Cozy's front desk uses, working from the note: the case gets an owner, and each promise gets a day. A short fast forward then follows the case through shift change, the private talk with Dan, the member who made the call, and the call back to Sofia on the day promised. Sofia gives 5 out of 5 after that promise is kept.
  • Less support as skill grows. In the course, the learner picks lines from a set of options. In three practice activities after the course, they write their own. This follows the expertise reversal effect: support that helps a beginner gets in the way as skill grows. Two of the three use AI. In the practice call, Omar, a member, calls about noise in Flex: an AI plays Omar, and a second AI, the grader, checks the call against the criteria. The two are separate, so a friendly member cannot make a call look better than it was. The second practice is the private talk with Dan, a short exercise of five questions. In the written practice, the learner writes a log entry from a note about Marta, a member whose labeled lunch was thrown out, and the grader checks the entry. The practices do not count toward passing, because AI scoring can vary. Three job aids hold the minimum information from the action map: the HEARD card, the Options card, which lists the options staff can offer, and the log card. The HEARD and Options cards become available after the first ending, so the learner first meets the problem without them, and the log card becomes available in Part 2. Each card can be printed from the course.
  • The analysis is in this case study. The design document sets out how the course works and why, and the storyboard holds every line and screen of the course. The style guide sets out the look of the screens and of the Cozy brand.
  • The course is a web app. React draws the screens, XState runs the course's logic, Zod checks the content and Motion runs the animations, and Vercel hosts the app. All the words of the course are kept in one content file, and an automated test checks every line against the storyboard, word for word.
  • Sofia's lines are synthetic speech. The faces come from Open Peeps, a free set of hand-drawn people by Pablo Stanley, and the DiceBear library draws them. The fonts are Archivo and IBM Plex, and the sounds were made for the course.
  • The practice call and the written practice run through a small server function on Vercel. If the AI service cannot be reached, they show a model call or model entries, so the learner can still compare their own words with a good answer.
  • Progress is saved in the browser after every step, so a learner who is interrupted on shift can come back to the same place. In a rollout at a real company, the same results would go to a learning management system (LMS), the software companies use to give courses to staff and record their results.
  • Automated unit tests check the course's logic and content, including the check of every line against the storyboard. Automated browser tests go through the whole course, as a learner would, on a desktop, a laptop and a phone. They also check that every button is large enough to tap and that the course fits a phone 320 pixels wide, and other tests check the contrast of the colors.
  • The built course had an expert review, screen by screen, on a desktop, laptops and a phone. The review took three points of view: a strict reviewer of user experience, Jane, and a new hire who knows nothing about Cozy or the course.
  • The storyboard had a cognitive walkthrough, in which the reviewer goes through each moment as a learner and asks whether the learner would know what to do next. It was done once as Jane and once as a new hire.

If the log reaches 9 in 10 but the complaint score does not rise past 3.2 after the first quarter, staff are following up on time. The next question is then whether the options they offer, such as a desk in Focus or a private talk with the other member, meet members' needs.

The next step would be to test the course with real front-desk staff on their own devices during a shift, before a wider rollout. They would think aloud, saying what they think as they go, and I would watch where they hesitate.
