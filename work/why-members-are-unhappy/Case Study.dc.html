A root-cause analysis found three reasons Cozy’s members were unhappy, and a solution for each one.

For complaints, Hear Them Out, a scenario-based course for front-desk staff, comes with an updated issue log that gives every case a next step, an owner and a date. For the space rules, signs and a first-day guide show members the rules where they work. For Wi-Fi outages, a checklist helps staff handle every outage the same way.

In one year, overall member satisfaction fell from 4.1/5 to 3.2/5. The goal is to raise it to 4.0/5, and the complaint score from 2.7/5 to 3.8/5, within two quarters.

Cozy’s members were growing less satisfied, and they gave the way Cozy handles complaints the lowest score of all.

Cozy rents desks and rooms in three coworking spaces to freelancers, remote workers and small teams from many countries. Each space has three zones: Focus is silent, Flex is for desk work, calls and meetings, and Private has closed rooms. Front-desk staff, whom Cozy calls community managers, welcome members, handle complaints and run events in shifts, with 20 to 30 minutes for learning in quiet periods.

In one year, overall member satisfaction fell from 4.1/5 to 3.2/5, and the score for how Cozy handles complaints became the lowest in the member survey, at 2.7/5. Members felt unheard and, at times, treated unfairly, and many complaints were never followed up on or resolved. Three problems came up the most:

  • Complaint handling
  • Members breaking space rules in Focus and the kitchen
  • Wi-Fi outages

The issue log, where staff write down complaints, shows the first: only about half of the complaints have a written next step, and some members waited weeks for a reply. The quarterly member survey shows the other two. Its comments name noise and calls in Focus, the silent zone, the shared kitchen and fridge, and Wi-Fi outages.

“We’ll look into it.”

A typical vague reply members heard whenever they called to complain

Two groups have to act differently:

Members need to follow the space rules, such as no calls in Focus and a name and a date on food in the fridge. They see the rules once, in the handbook they get when they join.
Front-desk staff handle outages and complaints. Jane, the persona, is based on the shift interviews and the issue log. On a complaint call, she explains the Focus rule before asking what happened, offers a quick fix and hands open cases over by word of mouth.

Given this, Cozy’s board set a goal to raise member satisfaction, especially the complaint score.

Specifically, within two quarters, overall satisfaction should rise from 3.2/5 to 4.0/5, and the complaint score from 2.7/5 to 3.8/5. Each week, managers check that 9 in 10 complaints have a dated next step.

Cozy’s quarterly member survey measures both scores, from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied).

With the Behavior Engineering Model (BEM), I found three root causes:

  1. 1
    No follow-up process or call practice for complaints
  2. 2
    Space rules members could not see
  3. 3
    No set steps for Wi-Fi outages

No follow-up process or call practice for complaints

BEM cells: information + tools + knowledge

Public reviews of coworking spaces describe promises with no follow-up. At Cozy, the issue log asks for no follow-up date and no owner, and open cases pass between shifts by word of mouth, so following up depends on memory. That part of the cause is a gap in information and tools. In the shift interviews, staff answered one question:

“A member calls, upset about noise. What do you say?”

Shift interviews · the question

They explained the rules before asking what happened, apologized in general terms and ended with “we’ll look into it”. Staff could write a date if the log asked for one, but calming an upset member and agreeing on a solution take practice, so that part needs a course.

Space rules members could not see

BEM cells: information

Noise is the most common dislike in Deskmag’s global survey of coworking members, for about 1 in 4 of them. At Cozy, members are unsure which zone they are in and when the fridge is cleared, and the visit showed why: Focus and Flex look the same, and the rules sit in the handbook. That is a gap in information. Members could follow the rules if they had to, so they need to see them where they work.

No set steps for Wi-Fi outages

BEM cells: information + tools

In Deskmag’s first survey, 99% of members named internet access as the service they need most. At Cozy, each shift handles an outage in its own order, most wait for the cause before telling members, and each decides compensation on its own. Staff also switch to the backup line by hand. That is a gap in information and tools: staff can do every step, but outages are rare and stressful, so a checklist keeps the steps at hand, and the backup line should switch on by itself.

I matched each root cause with a solution:

  1. 1
    A follow-up process and call practice for complaints
  2. 2
    Space rules members can see
  3. 3
    Set steps for Wi-Fi outages

Choosing the kind of solution. The space rules and the Wi-Fi outages are gaps in information and tools, so signs and a checklist fit them. The complaint cause needs both a change to the log and a course, which start in the same quarter.

A follow-up process and call practice for complaints

For front-desk staff and managers

The log changes. Cozy’s managers make the follow-up date and the owner required in the log, add a reminder on that date and a check of open cases at each shift change, and confirm which options staff may offer. Every week, they check the log for missed follow-ups, and the same check tracks the 9 in 10 target. Structured logs based on checklists prevent failures at handover (ASM Consortium, 2011). The log card, a job aid from the course, shows staff what a complete entry holds.

Hear Them Out. The course gives front-desk staff call practice, in about 20 minutes on a computer or a phone. The next section describes it.

Space rules members can see

For members

Prompts work best where people act: at recycling bins, specific prompts raised correct recycling by 54%, against 17% for the same prompts 4 meters away (Austin et al., 1993). So a sign marks where Focus starts, colored tape marks the edge of each zone, and labels in the kitchen show how the fridge and the shelves work. Each one says what to do and what to do instead, with one icon, word and color for each zone. Cozy in 60 seconds, eight short screens in the Cozy app, shows the zones and the signs on a member’s first day.

Set steps for Wi-Fi outages

For front-desk staff and operations

The Wi-Fi Down card, a one-page checklist at the front desk, gives every shift the same seven steps: check the scope, tell everyone within 10 minutes, get the Wi-Fi back, send an update every 15 minutes, call the head of operations at 30 minutes, thank everyone and log the outage. A wait with no known end feels longer, so the first notice goes out before the cause is known (Maister, 1985). Everyone present gets the same compensation on every shift, because a procedure feels fair when it is applied the same way (Leventhal, 1980). The head of operations also makes the switch to the backup line automatic.

Open the four job aid cards

With action mapping, I chose five actions that resolve a complaint, and Hear Them Out practices all five in one call and its follow-up.

Action mapping (Moore, 2017) set the five actions, from hearing the member out to calling back on the promised day, and sent barriers in the work to the managers instead. Sofia, a member, calls because another member took a half-hour call next to her in Focus, two weeks after she reported calls there. The call follows HEARD, a service recovery technique taught by the Disney Institute, with one decision for each step: Hear, Empathize, Apologize, Resolve and Diagnose. Son, a guide, describes her mood after each reply, because on the phone the learner cannot see her face. Following Merrill’s first principles and Knowles’s andragogy, the course starts with a call from the job and leaves the rules for later. It takes about 20 minutes on a computer or a phone, and progress is saved after every step.

  • Part 1, the call. Five decisions, one for each step of HEARD. Each weaker option holds one mistake, and several are the habits the shift interviews found.
  • If a decision went wrong. A week later, Sofia scores Cozy in the member survey: 3/5, 2/5 or 1/5, depending on how the call felt (decisions 1 to 3) and whether her problem was solved (4 and 5). A debrief marks each decision, and the learner rewinds to the first one that went wrong. If the learner hangs up on Sofia twice, the call starts again.
  • If all five went right. The learner moves on to Part 2: logs the case in Cozy Desk from their own call note, then the course moves ahead to the call back on the promised day. Sofia scores Cozy 5/5.
  • After the course. The learner can practice more if they want, in three ways: a new call, a private talk and a log entry to write. The new call is with Omar, a member played by an AI model and checked by a second one. Three job aids, the HEARD, Options and log cards, hold what the learner needs to know.

The action map, the branch map of the call and the reason for each design choice are in the design document.

Open the design document

I wrote the lines and screens in a text-based storyboard first, then designed the look of the course and the Cozy brand.

I began with a mood board of coworking spaces, phone call screens and front-desk software, since the course happens on a phone and a desk computer. I then set the look in a style guide: a dark ground, light paper boxes, an amber lamp color and faces with a word for each mood. Next, I made mockups of the 16 screens and a clickable prototype with five decisions, one for each step of HEARD, and refined them after the first round. The storyboard holds every line of the call, its endings and the debrief, and the style guide sets the look of the course, Cozy Desk and Cozy’s job aids and first-day guide.

Open the storyboardOpen the style guide

I built Hear Them Out as a web app, so it runs in a browser on a computer or a phone.

The course logic works like an Articulate Storyline project, with states, variables and triggers. Each step of the course is a state, variables keep the learner’s choices, which decide the score Sofia gives Cozy in the survey, and triggers move the learner to the next step.

In code, React draws the screens, XState runs these states and triggers, Zod checks the content and Motion runs the animations. Sofia’s lines are synthetic speech, also shown on screen, and in a practice call after the course, an AI model plays Omar, a member. The course is hosted on Vercel. A test checks every line of the course against the storyboard, word for word. Browser tests run through the course on a desktop, a laptop and a phone, to make sure it works smoothly on each. Progress is saved after every step, so a learner who is interrupted on shift comes back to the same place.
