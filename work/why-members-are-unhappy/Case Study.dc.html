In one year, Cozy's member satisfaction fell from 4.1 to 3.2 out of 5, and "How Cozy handles complaints" became its lowest score, at 2.7. The analysis traced the drop to three causes, and only one of them is a skill gap. The space rules sat in a handbook, so the fix puts them where members work: signs in the space, and Cozy in 60 seconds on a new member's first day. Each shift handled Wi-Fi outages its own way, so a one-page checklist sets one order of steps and one compensation rule. Complaint handling needed two fixes: managers make the follow-up date and the owner required in the log, and a branching course, Hear Them Out, builds the conversation and the follow-through. The goal is to bring satisfaction back to 4.0, and the complaint score up to 3.8, within two quarters.

Cozy is a coworking business with three spaces. Its members are freelancers, remote workers and small startup teams from many countries. They pay for one of two monthly plans. A hot desk plan gives any free desk in Focus or Flex, and a room in Private by the hour. A dedicated plan gives a room of one's own in Private, plus any free desk in Focus or Flex. Each space has three work zones: Focus is silent; Flex is for desk work, calls and meetings; Private has enclosed rooms. Each space also has meeting rooms and a shared kitchen.

Community managers run the front desks in shifts. They welcome new members, answer questions and complaints, run events and handle plan changes. In quiet periods, they can learn for 20 to 30 minutes a shift, on the front desk computer or their phone. A head of operations manages the buildings, the internet (a main line and a backup line at each space) and the suppliers.

Member comments name three problems: noise and phone calls in Focus, the shared kitchen and fridge, and Wi-Fi outages. The survey and the issue log add a fourth: how complaints are handled. About half of the complaints in the log have a next step, and several members waited weeks for a reply.

Members follow the space rules: no calls in Focus, and a name and a date on food in the fridge. They meet the rules once, in the handbook they get when they join, and they bring different habits about noise and sharing from many cultures.
Community managers handle outages and complaints. Jane is typical. She has three years at Cozy, knows most members by name and wants calls short. She studies in the quiet hour after lunch and is often interrupted. On a complaint call, she explains the Focus rule before asking what happened, offers a quick fix such as earplugs or a free coffee, and hands open cases over by word of mouth.

Noise in Focus and the shared kitchen. In Deskmag's global coworking survey, noise is the most common dislike: about 1 in 4 members are annoyed by it, though they rate their space only a little lower than others do (8.2 against 8.5 out of 10). At Cozy, survey comments show members unsure which zone they are in, what its rules are, and when the fridge is cleared. The walk-through shows why: on the floor, Focus and Flex look the same. The rules are clear, but they sit in the handbook, far from where members act. That is an information gap at the point of action (Data). Members could follow the rules if they had to; they need to see them where they work.

Wi-Fi outages. In Deskmag's first survey, 99% of members named internet access as the service they need most. At Cozy, shift interviews and the outage log show each shift working in its own order: some switch to the backup line first, some call the provider first. Most wait for the cause before telling members, and each shift decides compensation by feel. New staff learn by shadowing a colleague for two days, so each shift keeps its own habits, and the switch to the backup line is manual. That is a gap in information and tools (Data, Instruments). Staff can do every step, but outages are rare and stressful, so the order slips. That calls for a checklist at hand.

Complaint handling. Public reviews of coworking spaces describe promises with no follow-up and tickets with no reply. In Deskmag's survey, liking the people who run the space is the second strongest lift to member ratings, after liking the other members. At Cozy, the issue log leaves the follow-up date optional, open cases have no named owner, and handover at shift change is verbal. So follow-through depends on memory, and managers cannot see it (Data, Instruments, Incentives). In shift interviews, staff answered one scenario:

"A member calls, upset about noise. What do you say?"

Shift interviews · the scenario

They explained the rules before asking what happened, gave a general apology, and ended with "we'll look into it" (Knowledge). Staff could write a date if they had to, so the log needs to ask for it. Calming an upset member, owning Cozy's part and agreeing on a fix that matches the need is different: it takes practice.

Training only where it pays. Two of the three causes sit in the environment, so information and tools fix them, faster and for less than a course. Chapman's benchmarks put a half-hour branching course at roughly 90 to 360 development hours, while a checklist and a set of signs take days. The third cause needs both a process change and training, and it matters most. Noise lowers ratings only a little, while how members feel about the people running the space moves ratings a lot, and complaint handling is Cozy's lowest score. So the course is the center of the project, with the guide and the card beside it.

Cozy in 60 seconds and the signs, for members

The rules move to where the behavior happens. In one study, specific prompts placed right at the bins raised correct recycling by 54%, against 17% for the same prompts 4 meters away (Austin et al., 1993). So the signs go in the space:

  • a Focus sign where Focus starts: "Silent zone. Calls and meetings in Flex", with an arrow in Flex's color
  • the zone map in colored tape along the zone edges, and on cards on the desks at the edge of Focus
  • in the kitchen: fridge labels with a name and a date, a "Help yourself" shelf, the Friday 6 pm clear-out, a sign for cups, and a message for leftover event food

Cozy in 60 seconds shows all of this on a new member's first day. It is a short, phone-first guide in the Cozy app: the zone map, where to take a call, how the kitchen works, and the signs they will meet, so they know them on the floor. It starts by asking how the member works, because what matters most in 60 seconds differs for someone on calls all day and someone who needs deep focus.

Four rules hold every piece together: place it where the behavior happens; say exactly what to do, with the alternative right next to it; use one system of icon, word and color; and show it from day one. Focus stays fully silent, so members get a quiet zone they can count on. In open-plan offices, uncontrollable noise and lost privacy are the main sources of dissatisfaction (Kim and de Dear, 2013).

The Wi-Fi Down card, for staff

A one-page READ-DO checklist: the community manager reads a step, does it, then goes on. Job aids fit tasks that are rare, complex or costly to get wrong (Rossett and Schafer, 2007). The seven steps:

  1. 1
    Check the scope, in one minute.
  2. 2
    Send the first notice within 10 minutes, before the cause is known, with no fix time promised.
  3. 3
    Switch to the backup line, restart, then call the provider.
  4. 4
    Update every 15 minutes, even with nothing new.
  5. 5
    Call the head of operations after 30 minutes.
  6. 6
    When it is back, compensate.
  7. 7
    Log it.

Each step names the mistake to avoid, and the card holds the messages to send.

The timing comes from how waiting feels: an unexplained wait of unknown length feels longer than one with a promised update (Maister, 1985). Incident teams communicate early, at a set pace, and own the problem even when a provider caused it (Atlassian Statuspage). Compensation is the same for everyone present, on every shift, because people judge a procedure fair when it is applied consistently (Leventhal, 1980). Everyone present is offered a drink at the first notice. For an outage of 30 minutes or more, everyone present also gets one free hour in a bookable room within 30 days, which matches what members lost: working time (Roschk and Gelbrich, 2014). The analysis also asks the head of operations to make the switch to the backup line automatic, with an alert.

Hear Them Out and the log workflow, for staff

The managers' changes. In the first quarter, managers do four things:

  • make the follow-up date and the owner required in the log
  • add a handover step that goes through open cases at shift change
  • add a reminder on the follow-up date
  • confirm the options staff may offer on the spot

Every week, they check the log for missed follow-ups and cases left open, and the same check tracks the 9 in 10 target. Hotel front-line staff say written instructions and support from colleagues help them recover from service failures (Schumacher and Komppula, 2016), and structured, checklist-based logs prevent handover failures (ASM Consortium, 2011).

The course. Hear Them Out teaches the conversation and the follow-through, in about 20 minutes, on the front desk computer or a phone. An action map links each thing the community manager does on the job to its practice and to the minimum information it needs. The barriers that belong to the environment go to managers instead (Moore's action mapping, with Gilbert's model).

The learner is the community manager on shift. Sofia, a member, calls the front desk, upset. Yesterday another member took a half-hour call next to her desk in Focus, and two weeks ago she left a note about calls in Focus that went into the log with no owner and no follow-up. Son, a guide, stays beside the learner through the course, and reads Sofia's mood after each choice, since a phone hides her face.

  • One call, in real time (Part 1). Five decisions, one per step of HEARD (Hear, Empathize, Apologize, Resolve, Diagnose), with a few lines to tap and say between them. Each weak option holds one real mistake taken from the shift interviews, such as the rule before the question, or a promise with no day. The best apology says exactly what Cozy got wrong (Lewicki, Polin and Lount, 2016), and names stay private on both sides (ISO 10002). Sofia answers in her own voice, and her mood shifts. The course starts with the call, not with rules (Merrill's first principles).
  • Endings the learner can see. Two axes, how the call felt and whether the fix came with a day, give one of four answers to Sofia's member survey a week later: the same question as the course goal. Customers judge a complaint by the outcome, the process and how they were treated (Tax, Brown and Chandrashekaran, 1998). Below 5/5, a debrief names the first decision that went wrong and rewinds to it, keeping the right ones before it (mastery learning).
  • From the note to the log (Part 2). The learner keeps a note during the call, then logs the case in Cozy Desk, a practice copy of the front desk software, from that note alone: an owner, and a day for each promise. A short fast forward follows the case through shift change, the talk with the other member and the call back on the day promised. The 5/5 survey answer comes only after the promise is kept.
  • Support that fades. In the course, the learner picks lines; in three practices after it, they produce them. In a new call, an AI plays Omar and a separate AI grades the call. A private talk with the member who made the call follows, then a log entry to write, checked by an AI (expertise reversal). Three job aids (HEARD, Options and the log card) hold the minimum information, and each prints from the course.
  • I wrote the analysis, the design document and the storyboard with Claude, and made the designs and the Cozy brand in Claude Design.
  • The course is a web app built with Claude Code in React, XState and Zod, and hosted on Vercel. All the course's words live in one content file, and a test checks every line against the storyboard, word for word.
  • Sofia's voice is made with Gemini text-to-speech. The faces are Open Peeps, drawn with DiceBear. The fonts are Archivo and IBM Plex, and the sounds are original.
  • The practices use two separate AIs: one plays the member, and one grades the call, so a friendly member cannot make a call look better than it was.
  • Progress is saved in the browser, so the course survives an interruption on shift. In a rollout, the same results would go to an LMS.
  • Unit tests, including the word-for-word check of every line against the storyboard, and browser tests on a desktop, a 1280 by 720 laptop and a 375-pixel-wide phone with touch.
  • Expert review of the built course, screen by screen, on a desktop, laptops and a phone, through three views: a strict UX reviewer, Jane, and a new hire who knows nothing about Cozy or the course.
  • A cognitive walkthrough of the storyboard as Jane and as a new hire, moment by moment.

If the log reaches 9 in 10 but the complaint score stalls at the first-quarter checkpoint, the next question is whether the fixes offered are worth anything to members.

Test the course with real front-desk staff, thinking aloud on their own shift devices, and watch where they hesitate before a wider rollout.
