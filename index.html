Why Members Are Unhappy

This project started from a drop in member satisfaction at Cozy, a business that rents desks and rooms in shared workspaces, known as coworking spaces. I looked for the causes of the drop and chose a fix for each one. One of the fixes is Hear Them Out, a course that I designed and built for Cozy’s front-desk staff, who handle member complaints. It is a branching scenario: the learner handles a complaint call, and each choice they make changes what happens next. Cozy and its data are fictional and were created for this portfolio. The public research they are based on is real.

Try the course ↗

THE PROBLEM

Cozy runs three coworking spaces, where freelancers, remote workers and small teams, called members, rent desks and rooms on monthly plans. Each space has three zones. Focus is a silent zone, Flex is for desk work, calls and meetings, and Private has closed rooms. Front-desk staff, called community managers at Cozy, work in shifts. They welcome members, answer their questions and handle their complaints.

Every quarter, Cozy runs a member survey with scores from 1 to 5. Over one year, overall satisfaction fell from 4.1 to 3.2. The lowest score in the survey, 2.7, was for how Cozy handles complaints. In their comments, members wrote about three problems in the spaces: noise and phone calls in Focus, the shared kitchen and fridge, and Wi-Fi outages. The issue log, where staff record complaints, pointed to a fourth problem, the way complaints are handled. About half of the complaints in the log had a written next step, meaning what Cozy would do about it, and several members had waited weeks for a reply.

THE GOAL

I set three targets for the next two quarters. Overall satisfaction should rise from 3.2 to 4.0 and reach 3.5 after the first quarter. The score for how Cozy handles complaints should rise from 2.7 to 3.8. Because the survey runs once a quarter, the third target is a measure that Cozy’s managers can check every week in the issue log. For 9 in 10 complaints, the log should show a next step with a follow-up date, and the member should hear back from Cozy by that date.

FINDING THE CAUSES

Before choosing any fix, I looked for the cause of each problem. The evidence came from the survey comments, the issue log, the outage log, interviews with staff from each shift, and a visit to one of the spaces. This data is fictional, and it is based on real public sources, which I also used as evidence: surveys of coworking members, public reviews of coworking spaces, and studies of how businesses handle complaints.

To decide what kind of problem each cause was, I used the Six Boxes, Carl Binder’s version of the Behavior Engineering Model by Thomas Gilbert. It lists six things that shape what people do at work. Three are in the environment: the information, tools and incentives people have. Three are in the person: their knowledge, their motives, and their capacity, meaning whether they are able to do the work. I checked the environment first, because changing it usually costs less than training people. I also asked a question from Robert Mager and Peter Pipe’s book on performance problems: could people do the task if they had to? If they could, the cause is not a lack of skill, and a course would not fix it.

Noise and the kitchen had the same cause, so the four problems come down to three causes. Each row below gives what I found, the kind of cause and the fix.

Noise and the kitchen

INFORMATION
WHAT I FOUND

In the survey comments, members were unsure which zone they were in, what its rules were and when the fridge was cleared. During the visit, Focus and Flex looked the same, and the rules were written in the handbook that members get when they join.

THE FIX

Members could follow the rules if they had to. They need to see the rules where they work, so the fix is signs in the space and a short guide for a new member’s first day.

Wi-Fi outages

INFORMATION + TOOLS
WHAT I FOUND

In the outage log and the interviews, each shift took the steps of handling an outage in a different order. Most shifts waited until they knew the cause before telling members, and each shift decided on its own what to give members to make up for an outage. Cozy has a backup internet line at each space, and staff switched to it by hand.

THE FIX

Staff could do each step if they had to, but outages are rare and stressful, so steps get done in the wrong order. The fix is a checklist at the front desk and an automatic switch to the backup line.

Complaint handling

ENVIRONMENT + KNOWLEDGE
WHAT I FOUND

The issue log did not ask for a follow-up date or for an owner, the person responsible for the case. Cases that were still open were passed to the next shift by word of mouth. In the interviews, when asked what they would say to an upset member, staff explained the rules first, apologized in general terms and said they would look into it.

THE FIX

Staff could write a date if they had to, so the log should ask for it. Calming an upset member, admitting what Cozy got wrong and agreeing on a fix take practice, so that part needs a course. The fix is a change to the log and a course for front-desk staff.

THE FIXES

I matched each fix to its cause and to the people who can act on it. The first two causes were gaps in information and tools, so their fixes are signs, a guide, a checklist and an automatic switch to the backup line. The third cause was in the log and in the way staff handle upset members, so its fix is a change to the log, which Cozy’s managers make, and a course for front-desk staff.

Signs and Cozy in 60 seconds

FOR MEMBERS

Signs go up where each rule applies. A sign marks where Focus starts, colored tape marks the edges of each zone, and labels in the kitchen show how to use the fridge and the shelves. Cozy in 60 seconds is a guide of eight short screens in the Cozy app. On a new member’s first day, it shows the zones, where to take a call and how the kitchen works.

Wi-Fi Down card

FOR FRONT-DESK STAFF AND OPERATIONS

The Wi-Fi Down card is a job aid, a one-page checklist kept at the front desk. During an outage, staff read each step and do it before going on to the next. The card tells staff to inform members within 10 minutes without waiting for the cause, and to send an update every 15 minutes. It also sets the same compensation for every member, a free hour in a room after an outage of 30 minutes or more. The head of operations, who manages the buildings and the internet, makes the switch to the backup line automatic.

Log changes and Hear Them Out

FOR MANAGERS AND FRONT-DESK STAFF

Cozy’s managers make the follow-up date and the owner required fields in the log, and add a reminder on the follow-up date. They add a step at each shift change to go through the cases that are still open. They also confirm which options staff may offer an upset member, such as a desk in Focus or a private talk with another member. Hear Them Out is a course of about 20 minutes that teaches front-desk staff to handle a complaint call and to follow the case through in the log. The log changes and the course start in the same quarter.

THE COURSE: HEAR THEM OUT

I designed the course for front-desk staff, using a persona called Jane. A persona is a made-up but typical learner, and I based Jane on the shift interviews and the issue log. Jane has worked at Cozy for three years and knows most members by name. She studies in the quiet hour after lunch, at the front-desk computer or on her phone, and is often interrupted. So each part of the course takes 15 minutes or less, about 20 minutes in all. The course works on a computer and on a phone, and it saves progress after every step.

To decide what the course should teach, I used action mapping, a method for planning training created by Cathy Moore. An action map starts from the business goal and lists what people need to do to reach it. Here, it lists what front-desk staff must do on a complaint call and after it, and gives each action a practice activity and the information staff need for it. When something at work keeps staff from doing an action, such as a log that does not ask for a date, Cozy’s managers fix it instead of the course.

The course starts with a complaint call from a member, Sofia, because a realistic case lets learners see how a member reacts to their usual way of handling complaints. Sofia calls because yesterday another member took a half-hour phone call next to her in Focus, the silent zone. She had reported calls in Focus two weeks before, and nobody had followed up.
Before answering the phone, the learner starts a note of the call. During the call, they can add any line of the conversation to the note, because taking notes is part of the job. Later, the learner uses the note to record the case in the log.
The call has five decisions, one for each step of HEARD, a way of handling complaints taught by the Disney Institute, Disney’s training company. The five steps are Hear, Empathize, Apologize, Resolve and Diagnose. Each decision has a best option and weaker ones, and each weaker option contains one mistake, such as telling Sofia the other member’s name or promising to act without saying when.
Sofia replies to every choice, and her mood changes with each reply. A guide character named Son stays with the learner through the course and describes her mood after each reply, because on a phone call you cannot see the member’s face. He never gives a hint before a first try, so the learner makes each first choice without help.
Every path ends with Sofia’s score in the member survey. The score depends on two things: whether the learner listened, showed empathy and apologized well in decisions 1 to 3, and whether decisions 4 and 5 solved her problem. That gives four possible scores, 5, 3, 2 or 1 out of 5, and a score below 5 shows which of the two parts went wrong, or that both did. I used survey scores as endings because they tie each call to the course goal, the score for how Cozy handles complaints.
If any decision went wrong, the course jumps ahead a week once the call is over and shows Sofia’s score. Then a debrief, a review of the call, marks each decision as right or not yet right. The learner can rewind to the first decision that went wrong, and the right choices before it are kept, so the learner practices the part they have not mastered yet.
If all five decisions went right, Sofia hangs up and waits to see whether Cozy follows up, and Part 2 begins. Using their note, the learner records the case in Cozy Desk, a practice copy of the software Cozy’s front desk uses. The course then moves ahead in time. At shift change, the case is on the list of open cases, so the next shift can see it. On the next working day, the learner talks privately with the member who made the call in Focus, and on the day after, they call Sofia back as promised. Sofia gives 5 out of 5 after that promise is kept.
Three job aids become available during the course. The HEARD card and the Options card, which lists the options staff can offer, become available after the first ending, so the learner first meets the problem without them. The Log card becomes available in Part 2.
After the course, the learner can try three practice activities: a new call with a member played by an AI, the private talk with the member who made the call in Focus, and a log entry to write, which an AI checks. The practice activities do not count toward passing. Two of them are checked by an AI, which can score the same answer differently, and the private talk is a short exercise of five questions.

HOW IT WAS BUILT AND TESTED

I wrote the case study, the design document and the storyboard with Claude, Anthropic’s AI assistant. The case study holds the analysis behind the fixes, the design document sets out how the course works and why, and the storyboard holds every line and screen of the course. I designed the screens and the Cozy brand in Claude Design. Then I built the course as a web app with Claude Code, Anthropic’s coding tool. React draws the screens, XState runs the course’s logic, Zod checks the content and Vercel hosts the app. Sofia’s voice is made with Gemini text-to-speech, and the AI in the practice activities runs on Gemini, Google’s AI model.

All the words of the course are kept in one content file, and an automated test checks every line against the storyboard, word for word. Automated browser tests go through the whole course, as a learner would, on a desktop, a laptop and a phone. They also check that every button is large enough to tap and that the course fits a phone 320 pixels wide, and other tests check the contrast of the colors. The built course had an expert review, screen by screen, from three points of view: a strict reviewer of user experience, Jane, and a new hire who knows nothing about Cozy. The storyboard had a cognitive walkthrough, in which the reviewer goes through each moment as a learner and asks whether the learner would know what to do next. It was done once as Jane and once as a new hire. The next step would be to test the course with real front-desk staff on their own devices during a shift, and to watch where they hesitate.

Not-Home Delivery Stop

This project started from a problem at a parcel company. At stops where nobody answers the door, fewer than half end with the parcel delivered on the driver’s first attempt. I mapped what drivers need to do at those stops and what keeps them from doing it, and chose a fix for each barrier. One of the fixes is Not-Home Delivery Stop, a short online course, or module, of seven slides, which I designed and built for drivers. It runs on the web and in a company’s learning management system. The parcel company and its numbers are fictional and were created for this portfolio.

Try the module ↗

THE PROBLEM

When a delivery driver rings the doorbell and nobody answers, the driver has to decide what to do with the parcel. It can go to a safe place at the house, to a neighbor, or to a pickup point, where the customer collects it later. The choice depends on the handling code on the parcel’s label, the stop note with the customer’s delivery instructions, and what the customer says when the driver calls.

There are three handling codes. An STD parcel can go to a neighbor or a pickup point, and to a safe place if the stop note or the customer on the phone allows it. A SIG parcel needs a signature, so it goes to a neighbor who signs, with the customer’s approval, or to a pickup point. An AGE parcel is age-restricted, so it goes to a pickup point, where the customer shows photo ID.

These stops are called not-home stops, and at the parcel company 45% of them end with the parcel delivered on the first attempt. A parcel that goes back to the depot needs another delivery trip. A parcel left in a place the rules do not allow can go missing, and the customer can then make a claim for it.

THE GOAL

I set two targets for the first three months after rollout. First-attempt success on not-home stops should rise from 45% to 65%. At the same time, the share of those stops that lead to a claim for a missing parcel should stay at 0.5% or less. Inside the module, a driver passes with 80 out of 100 points if neither of the two stops on the last slide ends with a broken rule.

FINDING THE CAUSES

To find what keeps drivers from succeeding at these stops, I used action mapping, a method for planning training created by Cathy Moore. An action map starts from the business goal and lists what people need to do to reach it. For each action, it names the barriers, meaning what keeps people from doing it, and asks whether training can remove each barrier or whether the way the work is set up should change. Each action also gets a practice activity and the information people need for it.

The map for this project has four actions and seven barriers. Each barrier is marked as a matter of motivation, knowledge, skill or the environment drivers work in. Two of the barriers come from the way the work is set up, so their fixes are changes in operations, and the module covers the other five. Each row below gives what gets in the way of an action, the kind of barrier and the fix.

Follow the steps in order

MOTIVATION + KNOWLEDGE
WHAT GETS IN THE WAY

Drivers need to follow the six steps of a not-home stop in order, including the call to the customer, before choosing where the parcel goes. Some drivers skip the call to protect their stop rate, one of the numbers on the scorecard that tracks each driver’s work. Some are unsure of the order.

THE FIX

Slide 1 practices the order, and a job aid, the Sequence card, lists the steps. The driver scorecard also changes to count handovers to a neighbor or a pickup point as first-attempt successes.

Choose an allowed option

KNOWLEDGE + ENVIRONMENT
WHAT GETS IN THE WAY

Drivers need to choose a place for the parcel that the handling code and the customer’s instructions allow, from the stop note or the call. They are unsure what SIG and AGE parcels allow, and stop notes are hard to find.

THE FIX

Slide 3 practices sorting parcels by code, slide 7 uses the same rules at two stops, and the Handling codes card says what each code allows. The screen drivers see when they arrive at a stop also changes to show the handling code and the stop notes.

Agree on an option by phone

SKILL
WHAT GETS IN THE WAY

Drivers need to call the customer and agree on an allowed option within two minutes. On the phone, some drivers agree to requests the rules do not allow, and their calls drift away from the point.

THE FIX

Slide 4 is a phone call with recorded voices, and on slide 5 the driver types a conversation with a customer played by an AI. The Call moves card lists the three things to do on a call: say who you are and what is happening, explain the options the parcel allows, and confirm the plan and the proof of delivery.

Leave the parcel in a safe place

SKILL + KNOWLEDGE
WHAT GETS IN THE WAY

Drivers need to leave the parcel in a spot that meets the four safe-place rules and take a photo that shows the house number. They misjudge whether a spot is hidden, and their photos do not show the house number.

THE FIX

Slide 6 asks the driver to find the safe place in a drawing of a house, and the first stop on slide 7 asks for it again, with the photo. The Safe place card lists the four rules: hidden from the street, protected from rain, inside the property, and doors and paths clear.

THE FIXES

I matched each fix to its barriers and to the people who can act on them. The five barriers about knowledge and skill go to the module, and the information drivers need goes into four job aids, short reference cards with what each action needs. The two barriers about motivation and the environment need changes from operations, to the driver scorecard and to the screen drivers see at a stop.

The module

FOR DRIVERS

The module has seven slides. On six of them, the driver practices the four actions: putting the steps in order, sorting parcels, two calls with customers, finding the safe place, and two stops where each choice changes what happens next. On the other slide, the driver opens the job aids, and a results screen at the end shows the score and the slides to try again.

Four job aids

FOR DRIVERS

The Sequence card lists the steps of a not-home stop, and the Handling codes card says what each code allows. The Safe place card lists the four rules for a safe place, and the Call moves card lists the three things to do on a call. Drivers open all four on slide 2, and from then on a Job aids button at the top of each slide opens them again.

Two changes in operations

FOR OPERATIONS

The screen drivers see when they arrive at a stop shows the handling code and the stop notes. The driver scorecard counts handovers to a neighbor or a pickup point as first-attempt successes, so a driver who calls the customer and hands the parcel over this way is not marked down.

THE MODULE: NOT-HOME DELIVERY STOP

Drivers use the module outdoors, on a phone, between stops. For that reason, each screen asks one question and has one main button, and the text is large with strong contrast. Each status, such as right or wrong, shows an icon and a word as well as a color. The slides follow the action map, and the job aids open on slide 2, so the practices on slides 3 to 7 have them at hand.

Slide 1 asks the driver to put the six steps of a not-home stop in order. A step can be dragged, moved with buttons, tapped into place or moved with the keyboard, so drivers who cannot drag can still do the task. After the driver checks the answer, each step in the wrong place says where it belongs and what goes wrong at a real stop in that order.
On slide 2, the driver opens the four job aids. On slide 3, the driver decides for each of six parcels whether it goes to a safe place, a neighbor or a pickup point, using its code, its stop note and what the customer said on the call.
Slide 4 is a phone call with Ms. Rivera, whose parcel needs a signature. The call has recorded voices and captions, and the sound plays when the driver presses Play. The call has up to three turns. At each turn, the driver picks one of three lines and hears her answer, and two of the lines end the call early. At the end, the slide shows which call moves the driver used.
On slide 5, the driver has a typed conversation with Mr. Hayes, a customer played by an AI. A second AI checks the conversation against the three call moves and quotes the driver’s words, and it does not see the instructions given to the first AI. The slide is not scored, because an AI can score the same answer differently. If the AI does not work, the slide switches to a self-check, where the driver reads a model call and marks the call moves they made.
Slide 6 shows a drawing of a house with five spots, and the driver picks the one that meets all four safe-place rules.
Slide 7 is a branching scenario with two stops, in which each choice changes what happens next. After each choice, the driver sees what happens, and the results screen then explains the rule behind each choice.
A driver passes the module with 80 out of 100 points if no stop on slide 7 ends with a broken rule. The Try again button reopens the slides that lost points and keeps the scores of the others. The module also comes as a SCORM 1.2 package. SCORM is a standard format for a learning management system (LMS), the software companies use to give courses to staff. With it, the LMS runs the module and saves each driver’s progress and best score.

HOW IT WAS BUILT AND TESTED

I built the module as a web app with Claude Code, Anthropic’s coding tool. React draws the screens, Vite builds the code, XState runs the logic of each slide, and Zod checks the content files, where the slides, the job aids and the action map are written. The voices on slide 4 are made with Piper, an open-source text-to-speech tool, and the captions are timed to the audio. The AI customer and the AI that checks the call on slide 5 run on Gemini, Google’s AI model. The module is hosted on Vercel, and GitHub Actions builds the SCORM package.

Automated unit tests check the logic of every slide, the scoring and the content. Automated browser tests try every slide with the keyboard alone and the drag-and-drop slides with a mouse and with touch, and one test goes through the whole module to a score of 100. An accessibility checker called axe-core tests every slide at four screen widths and in high-contrast modes. The SCORM package is tested in scorm-again, a stand-in for a real LMS. The next step would be to test the module with real drivers on their routes, since all of the testing so far is automated.
