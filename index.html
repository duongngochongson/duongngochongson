Why Members Are Unhappy

Cozy, a fictional coworking business, saw member satisfaction fall from 4.1 to 3.2 out of 5 in one year, and “How Cozy handles complaints” became its lowest score, at 2.7. I ran the needs analysis and traced the drop to three causes. Only one was a skill gap. So the fixes are signs and a first-day guide for members, an outage checklist for staff, and, for complaint calls, a change to the log for managers plus Hear Them Out, a branching course for the front desk.

THE PROBLEM

4.1 → 3.2
member satisfaction, in one year
2.7 / 5
how Cozy handles complaints, the lowest score
1 in 2
logged complaints that have a next step

On a complaint call, front-desk staff explained the rules before asking what happened, said sorry in general terms and ended with “we'll look into it”: no options and no date. Some members waited weeks for a reply.

WHAT THE ANALYSIS FOUND

I used the member survey, the issue and outage logs, shift interviews and a walk-through of the space, and sorted the causes with Gilbert's model. There were three causes, and only one was a skill gap.

The zone and kitchen rules sat in a handbook, far from where members work.

INFORMATION

Signs in the space, and Cozy in 60 seconds on a new member's first day.

Each shift handled Wi-Fi outages its own way, and the switch to the backup line was manual.

PROCEDURE + TOOL

The Wi-Fi Down card, and an automatic switch to the backup line.

Follow-up depended on memory, and staff handled upset members by habit.

PROCESS + SKILL

An owner and a follow-up date required in the log, and the course Hear Them Out.

Training only where it pays: two of the three causes are fixed with information and tools, not a course.

THE FIXES

Cozy in 60 seconds

FOR NEW MEMBERS

An eight-screen guide in the Cozy app: the zones, where to take a call, how the kitchen works and the signs they will see. The signs go up where each rule applies.

Wi-Fi Down card

FOR FRONT-DESK STAFF

A one-page READ-DO checklist: a first notice within 10 minutes, an update every 15 minutes, and one compensation rule for every shift.

Hear Them Out

FOR FRONT-DESK STAFF

A 20-minute branching course on complaint calls. At the same time, managers make the owner and the follow-up date required in the log.

THE COURSE: HEAR THEM OUT

It starts with the call, not the rules: the learner takes Sofia's complaint in real time and makes five decisions, one per step of HEARD (Hear, Empathize, Apologize, Resolve, Diagnose).
Every weaker option is a real habit from the shift interviews, like explaining the rule before asking, or promising a fix with no day.
Sofia's mood shifts line by line. A week later her survey says 1, 2, 3 or 5 out of 5, and a debrief rewinds to where the call turned.
In Part 2 the learner logs the case in Cozy Desk from their own call note, then follows it through to the call back on the day they promised.
Three practices follow: a new call with an AI-played member and a separate AI grader, the private talk with the member who made the noise, and a log entry to write, checked by an AI.

HOW IT WAS BUILT AND TESTED

I wrote the analysis, the design document and the storyboard with Claude, and made the designs and the Cozy brand in Claude Design. The course is a web app I built with Claude Code in React, XState and Zod, hosted on Vercel, and Sofia's voice is made with Gemini text-to-speech. A test checks every line against the storyboard, word for word. Browser tests run on a desktop, a 1280 × 720 laptop and a 375-pixel phone with touch, and an expert review and a cognitive walkthrough went through the course as Jane, a community manager with three years at Cozy, and as a new hire.

Not-Home Delivery Stop

A seven-slide e-learning module for city-route drivers, about what to do when nobody answers the door. The parcel company is fictional. I mapped what drivers need to do on the job, then built one practice for each action. The module runs on the web and ships as a SCORM 1.2 package for an LMS.

Open the module ↗

THE PROBLEM

45%
of not-home stops succeed on the first try
7
barriers behind it, across four driver actions
2 of 7
barriers that need a fix outside training

Drivers skip the call to protect their stop rate, aren't sure of the order of the steps, and aren't sure what SIG (signature) and AGE (age-check) parcels allow. On the phone they give in to requests outside the rules, like leaving a parcel that needs a signature at the door. At the house they misjudge hidden spots, and their photos often miss the house number.

WHAT THE ANALYSIS FOUND

The action map links the goal to four things drivers do on the job. Each action has its barriers, a practice and the minimum information it needs.

Follow the not-home sequence, including the call, before choosing an option.

MOTIVATION + KNOWLEDGE

Put the steps in order (slide 1) and the job aids (slide 2). The driver scorecard counts a neighbour handover as a success.

Choose an option the handling code and the customer's notes allow.

KNOWLEDGE + ENVIRONMENT

Sort the parcels (slide 3) and handle two stops (slide 7). The arrival screen shows the handling code and the stop notes.

Call the customer and agree on a permitted option within two minutes.

SKILL

A voiced phone call (slide 4) and a typed call with an AI customer (slide 5).

Leave the parcel in a spot that meets all four safe-place rules, with a photo that shows the house number.

SKILL + KNOWLEDGE

Find the safe place (slide 6) and handle two stops (slide 7).

Training only where it pays: two barriers go to operations instead of into the course.

THE FIXES

The module

FOR DRIVERS

Seven slides, with one practice for each action on the job, ending in two branching stops and a results screen.

Four job aids

FOR DRIVERS

The sequence, the handling codes, the safe place and the call moves. They open from every slide.

Two operations changes

FOR MANAGERS

The arrival screen shows the handling code and the stop notes, and the driver scorecard counts neighbour and pickup-point handovers as successes.

THE MODULE: NOT-HOME DELIVERY STOP

Each action has its own practice: drag the steps into order, sort parcels into bins, take a voiced phone call, pick the safe place on a drawing, and run two branching stops.
In slide 5 the learner types the call to Mr. Hayes, an AI customer, and a separate AI grader quotes the learner's own words against the three call moves.
The four job aids are the single source: the cards, the scoring, the AI prompts and the diagrams all read from them, so they can't drift apart.
Every drag has a keyboard and a tap alternative, every line has captions, and axe checks every slide at four screen widths.
It passes at 80 of 100 points with no policy violation, and the SCORM 1.2 package saves the progress and the result in the LMS.

HOW IT WAS BUILT AND TESTED

The module is built in React with Vite, with XState for each slide's logic and Zod to check the content. The voices in the call are made with Piper text-to-speech, with captions timed from the real audio. Unit tests cover the engine, browser tests click through every slide, and axe-core checks accessibility on every slide and state at 360, 600, 840 and 1280 pixels. The SCORM package is tested in a stand-in LMS.

About me

I teach, and I design like someone who has to stand in front of the room.

Teaching taught me something I never shook off: you know within ten minutes whether a session is working. I bring that instinct to learning design — and because I also come from data and business, I start from the problem and check the result with numbers.

WHAT I DO

I design learning experiences in code, so each one works on a phone, can be measured, and can be reused. Most of the job isn't making slides — it's working out what deserves to be taught, and saying so when training isn't the answer.

I'm comfortable sitting next to the numbers — SQL, Python, Power BI. Not for a nice report, but to know which part of a program to keep and which to cut.

WORKING WITH ME

I ask a lot of questions in week one. There's usually a different problem under the stated one.
I'd rather show you something rough and early than something polished and late.
I write in Vietnamese and English, and I can work with subject experts in both.
I hand over something your team can maintain — content kept in one place, tests, and a package your LMS can take.
