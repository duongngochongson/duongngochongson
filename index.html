Why Members Are Unhappy

Concept project

A root-cause analysis found three reasons why members of Cozy, a coworking business, were unhappy, and a solution for each one: a follow-up process and call practice for complaints, space rules members can see, and set steps for Wi-Fi outages. They are Hear Them Out, a scenario-based course for front-desk staff, with an updated issue log that gives every case a next step, an owner and a date; signs and a first-day guide that show members the space rules; and a Wi-Fi checklist that helps staff handle every outage the same way.

PROBLEM

Cozy’s members were growing less satisfied, and they gave the way Cozy handles complaints the lowest score of all.

They felt unheard and, at times, even treated unfairly. Many of their complaints were never followed up on or resolved. Three problems came up the most:

  • Complaint handlingSource: Issue log
  • Members breaking space rules in Focus and the kitchenSource: Survey
  • Wi-Fi outagesSource: Survey

GOAL

Given this, Cozy’s board set a goal to raise member satisfaction, especially the complaint score.

Specifically, within two quarters, overall satisfaction should rise from 3.2/5 to 4.0/5, and the complaint score from 2.7/5 to 3.8/5. Each week, managers check that 9 in 10 complaints have a dated next step. Now, about half have a written next step.

FINDING THE ROOT CAUSES

With the Behavior Engineering Model (BEM), I found three root causes:

  • No follow-up process or call practice for complaints
  • Space rules members could not see
  • No set steps for Wi-Fi outages
HOW I SORTED THE CAUSES

I sorted each cause into the BEM’s six cells. For each cause, I asked Mager and Pipe’s question: could people do it if they had to? If so, the fix is in the work around them. If not, practice helps. The evidence came from survey comments, the issue and outage logs, interviews with each shift, a visit to one space and public sources.

EnvironmentInformationToolsIncentivesPersonKnowledgeCapacityMotives
No follow-up process or call practice for complaintsINFORMATION + TOOLS + KNOWLEDGE

The log asked for no follow-up date or owner, and open cases passed between shifts by word of mouth. On calls, staff explained the rules first. Calming an upset member takes practice.

Space rules members could not seeINFORMATION

Focus and Flex, the zone for calls, looked the same, and the rules sat in the handbook. Members could follow the rules, so they need to see them where they work.

No set steps for Wi-Fi outagesINFORMATION + TOOLS

Each shift handled an outage in its own order, and most waited for the cause before telling members. Staff could do each step, but outages are rare and stressful.

SOLUTION

I matched each root cause with a solution:

A follow-up process and call practice for complaintsFOR FRONT-DESK STAFF AND MANAGERS

Hear Them Out trains staff to handle a complaint call. Managers make the follow-up date and the owner required in the log, with a reminder and a check at each shift change.

Space rules members can seeFOR MEMBERS

A sign, zone tape and kitchen labels, and Cozy in 60 seconds, eight short screens that show a new member the same rules.

Set steps for Wi-Fi outagesFOR FRONT-DESK STAFF AND OPERATIONS

A one-page checklist: tell members within 10 minutes, send an update every 15 minutes and give everyone the same compensation. The backup line also switches on by itself.

COURSE: HEAR THEM OUT

With action mapping, I chose five actions that resolve a complaint, and Hear Them Out practices all five in one call and its follow-up.

Sofia, a member, calls because another member took a half-hour call next to her in Focus, two weeks after she reported calls there. The call follows HEARD, a five-step way of handling complaints, with one decision for each step. Son, a guide, describes her mood after each reply, because on the phone the learner cannot see her face.

Following Merrill’s first principles and Knowles’s andragogy, the course starts with a call from the job and leaves the rules for later.

STORYBOARD & VISUAL DESIGN

I wrote the lines and screens in a text-based storyboard first, then designed the look of the course and the Cozy brand.

I began with a mood board of coworking spaces, phone call screens and front-desk software, since the course happens on a phone and a desk computer. I then set the look in a style guide: a dark ground, light paper boxes, an amber lamp color and faces with a word for each mood. Next, I made mockups of the 16 screens and a clickable prototype with five decisions, one for each step of HEARD, and refined them after the first round.

DEVELOPMENT

I built Hear Them Out as a web app, so it runs in a browser on a computer or a phone.

The course logic works like an Articulate Storyline project, with states, variables and triggers. Each step of the course is a state, variables keep the learner’s choices, which decide the score Sofia gives Cozy in the survey, and triggers move the learner to the next step.

In code, React draws the screens, XState runs these states and triggers, Zod checks the content and Motion runs the animations. Sofia’s lines are synthetic speech, also shown on screen, and in a practice call after the course, an AI model plays Omar, a member. Browser tests run through the course on a desktop, a laptop and a phone, to make sure it works smoothly on each.

If the log reaches 9 in 10 but the complaint score stays at 3.2/5, staff are following up on time, so the next question is whether the options they offer meet members’ needs.

The course measures Kirkpatrick’s levels 1 and 2, and the issue log and the member survey measure levels 3 and 4.

I had put a lot of time and effort into this project, and it taught me many lessons.

  • Plan slow, build fast. Planning every line and screen before building felt slow at first, but it saved a lot of time later, when I had to change things.
  • Plan for the unplanned. However carefully I planned, small issues still came up that I only found after building the prototype, so I now leave buffer time to fix them.
  • Go beyond drag and drop. Artificial intelligence is a great assistant. With it, I could build a voiced phone call and an open practice call, things that traditional drag-and-drop authoring tools make hard.

Not-Home Delivery Stop

Concept project

Discover Not-Home Delivery Stop, a mobile module where drivers practice the four actions an action map showed they need when nobody answers the door. Besides the module, other support tools help drivers deliver more parcels on the first attempt, so customers get them when they expect, with no rise in missing parcels: four job aids (to check the steps, the handling codes, the safe-place rules and what to say on a call) and two changes in operations (the handling code and stop notes on the arrival screen, and a scorecard that counts a neighbor or a pickup point as a success).

PROBLEM

At stops where nobody answers the door, too many parcels are not delivered on the first attempt.

When nobody answers the door, the driver decides where the parcel goes: a safe place, a neighbor or a pickup point. The choice depends on the handling code, the stop note and the customer’s answer on the phone, so it is easy to get wrong, and a wrong one means another trip or a missing parcel. Stop data shows two patterns: more than half of these parcels go back to the depot, often without a call to the customer, and some parcels that are left sit where people on the street can see them.

GOAL

Given this, the company set a goal to deliver more of these parcels on the first attempt, without more missing parcels.

Specifically, within three months, first-attempt success at not-home stops should rise from 45% to 65%, while claims for missing parcels stay at 0.5% or less of those stops.

FINDING THE ROOT CAUSES

With action mapping, I found seven barriers (five for training and two for operations) behind four key actions:

  • Follow the steps in order
  • Choose an allowed option
  • Agree on an option by phone
  • Leave the parcel in a safe place
HOW I SORTED THE BARRIERS

Cathy Moore sorts barriers into environment, knowledge, skill and motivation. Training helps with knowledge and skill. Stop notes that are hard to find, or a scorecard that rewards skipping the call, need a change in the work.

7 BARRIERSTrainingOperations
Follow the steps in orderMOTIVATION + KNOWLEDGE

Some drivers skip the call to protect their stop rate on the scorecard, and some are unsure of the order of the six steps.

Choose an allowed optionKNOWLEDGE + ENVIRONMENT

Drivers are unsure what SIG and AGE parcels allow, and stop notes are hard to find.

Agree on an option by phoneSKILL

Some drivers agree to requests the rules do not allow, and their calls drift from the point.

Leave the parcel in a safe placeSKILL + KNOWLEDGE

Drivers misjudge whether a spot is hidden, and their photos miss the house number.

SOLUTION

I matched the seven barriers with a solution in three parts:

Practice for the four actionsFOR DRIVERS

Not-Home Delivery Stop: seven slides on a phone, with one question per screen. Six practice the four actions, and one opens the job aids.

Job aids for what is hard to rememberFOR DRIVERS

The Sequence, Handling codes, Safe place and Call moves cards. Drivers open them on slide 2, and again from any later slide.

Two changes in operations for barriers in the work itselfFOR OPERATIONS

The arrival screen shows the handling code and the stop notes, and the scorecard counts a neighbor or a pickup point as a first-attempt success.

VISUAL DESIGN

I set the look of the module in a style guide built around one place: a phone, outdoors, between stops.

Three principles guide it: readable on the street (text of 16px or more, 7:1 contrast for body text, 44px controls), one thing per screen, and meaning never by color alone.

DEVELOPMENT

I built the module as a web app that also runs in a learning management system (LMS).

React draws the screens, XState runs each slide’s logic, and Zod checks the content files. Slide 4 has synthetic voices with captions, and on slide 5 an AI model plays a customer in a typed call.

Each change on GitHub builds a new SCORM 1.2 package, so an LMS can run the module and save each driver’s progress and best score. I tested the package in SCORM Cloud: it reported the score and the passed status correctly, and a learner who left halfway came back on the same slide, with the same scores.

About me

Hi, welcome here

I’m glad you found this part of the site. This page is about my own journey: what I like to do, and what drives me to become a learning designer.

So, what is this page about?

This page is basically about my career and personal-growth journey toward finding my ikigai in learning in general, and learning experience design in particular.

Long story short, it took a series of turning points for me to figure out what I believe is something I want to do in the long run. My journey took me through university, a change of major, and eventually into the working world, where I tried my hand at a few different fields. Each choice helped me narrow down what I genuinely enjoyed and what I was good at. Luckily, after a few turns, I started to see more and more clues. And I bet you can guess where those clues led me: instructional design, or learning experience design!

OMG, I’m genuinely passionate about it.

This is the kind of work where I can sit down and map out how a learning experience should unfold. I love pretty much every part of the learning design process. From digging into the problem and identifying learning needs and gaps, to deciding which learning activities should take priority, brainstorming the experience’s style and vibe, developing it, and finally choosing the right way to evaluate it.

On top of that, I think two things I’ve enjoyed since childhood have helped me in this field: tinkering with computers and technology, and having deep conversations that let me explore an idea with someone and really get to the bottom of it.

Fun fact: My name is Son, which means Sơn in Vietnamese. I usually go by Son without the diacritic to make things easier, since it’s still close to the original pronunciation.

  1. 2019 The first clue

    I started my journey into adulthood without really knowing what I liked, so I chose electrical and electronics engineering. It wasn’t the best experience, but at least it gave me my first clue: I needed to think more carefully about what I actually wanted to do.

  2. 2020 - 2024 Exploring different paths

    After that, I switched my major to Business Administration, and my university experience got a lot better. I had more opportunities to interact and communicate with others through my studies, and I gained a big-picture understanding of how businesses operate.

    When I explored marketing and data analysis, I developed valuable skills and ways of thinking. From planning and project management to looking at problems objectively, making data-driven decisions, and developing empathy for people, each experience gave me something useful to carry forward.

    Even so, something still felt missing. I wanted to do something I genuinely believed was meaningful.

  3. 2025 - 2026 Finding something meaningful

    And that’s where education - and instructional design in particular - came in!

    During my personal gap year, I worked with an NGO as a community worker and public-school educator. It turned out to be much more than just a job. The friends I made and the memories of living and working alongside others in rural Vietnam are things I’ll always carry with me.

    From a career perspective, the most valuable thing I discovered was just how much I enjoyed designing learning experiences and creating e-learning materials along the way.

    As an educator, I had to build lessons and training sessions from scratch. I started by identifying learning needs and gaps in the community where I worked. From there, I defined the program’s learning objectives and expected outcomes. I also had to adapt my approach and materials for different groups of learners, and I got to take part in evaluating the program afterward.

    I was genuinely happy to discover that my childhood curiosity about computers and technology made creating learning materials much easier. For example, I built interactive slide decks and used AI to vibe-code websites that helped learners understand concepts more easily.

    These experiences gave me a much more holistic picture of what designing a learning experience actually involves.

  4. 2026 - Now Looking for the next chapter

    As I look toward the next chapter of my career, I want to put my skills to work designing meaningful learning experiences.

    To me, a meaningful learning experience goes beyond simply delivering knowledge. It also needs to be designed around the learners themselves - their needs, characteristics, and contexts - in a way that genuinely engages them.

    I’m excited to work with teammates who are just as driven, and to figure out together what learning can make possible for organizations and their learners.

Outside of work, I enjoy watching movies 🎬, playing board games 🎲, and trying to figure out the secrets behind magic tricks 🪄. I guess I’ve always enjoyed exploring how things work, whether it’s a learning experience, a good story, or a clever illusion. If you’re into any of these, we might have something to talk about!
