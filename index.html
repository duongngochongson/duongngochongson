Why Members Are Unhappy

This project started from a drop in member satisfaction at Cozy, a coworking business. I looked for the causes of the drop and chose a fix for each one. One of the fixes is Hear Them Out, a branching scenario I designed and built for front-desk staff: the learner handles a complaint call, and each choice changes what happens next. Cozy and its data are fictional and were created for this portfolio, and they are based on real public research.

Try the course ↗

THE PROBLEM

Over one year, member satisfaction at Cozy fell from 4.1 to 3.2 out of 5, and the way Cozy handles complaints got the lowest score.

Cozy runs three coworking spaces, where freelancers, remote workers and small teams, called members, rent desks and rooms on monthly plans. Each space has three zones. Front-desk staff, whom Cozy calls community managers, work in shifts: they welcome members, answer their questions and handle their complaints.

  • FocusSilent work
  • FlexDesk work, calls and meetings
  • PrivateRooms with a door

Every quarter, Cozy runs a member survey with scores from 1 to 5. In their comments, members named three problems in the spaces. The issue log, where staff record complaints, pointed to a fourth problem in the way complaints are handled.

  • Noise and phone calls in Focus
  • The shared kitchen and fridge
  • Wi-Fi outages
  • Complaint handling: about half of the complaints had a written next step, and some members waited weeks for a reply

THE GOAL

Within two quarters, overall satisfaction should reach 4.0 and the score for how Cozy handles complaints should reach 3.8.

Because the survey runs once a quarter, I also chose a measure that Cozy’s managers can check every week in the issue log: 9 in 10 complaints should have a next step with a follow-up date, and the member should hear back by that date.

FINDING THE CAUSES

The four problems came down to three causes: two in the work environment, and one in the environment and in what staff knew how to do.

The evidence came from the survey comments, the issue log, the outage log, interviews with staff from each shift and a visit to one of the spaces. This data is fictional, so I based it on real public sources: surveys of coworking members, public reviews of coworking spaces and studies of how businesses handle complaints.

HOW I SORTED THE CAUSES

I used the Six Boxes, Carl Binder’s version of Thomas Gilbert’s Behavior Engineering Model. It lists six things that shape what people do at work, three in the environment and three in the person. I checked the environment first, because changing it usually costs less than training. For each cause, I also asked Robert Mager and Peter Pipe’s question: could people do the task if they had to? If they could, a course would not fix it.

Noise and the kitchen

INFORMATION
WHAT I FOUND

Members were unsure which zone they were in, what its rules were and when the fridge was cleared. Focus and Flex looked the same, and the rules sat in the handbook that members get when they join.

THE FIX

Members could follow the rules if they had to. They need to see the rules where they work, so the fix is signs in the space and a short guide for new members.

Wi-Fi outages

INFORMATION + TOOLS
WHAT I FOUND

Each shift handled an outage in a different order. Most waited until they knew the cause before telling members, and each shift decided compensation on its own. Staff switched to the backup internet line by hand.

THE FIX

Staff could do each step, but outages are rare and stressful, so steps get done in the wrong order. The fix is a checklist at the front desk and an automatic switch to the backup line.

Complaint handling

ENVIRONMENT + KNOWLEDGE
WHAT I FOUND

The log did not ask for a follow-up date or an owner, the person responsible for the case, and open cases were passed on by word of mouth. Asked what they would say to an upset member, staff explained the rules first, apologized in general terms and said they would look into it.

THE FIX

Staff could write a date if they had to, so the log should ask for it. Calming an upset member, admitting what Cozy got wrong and agreeing on a fix take practice, so the fix is a change to the log and a course.

THE FIXES

Each fix goes to the people who can act on its cause.

The first two causes needed information and tools, so their fixes are signs, a guide, a checklist and an automatic switch. The third needed a change to the log and practice for staff, so its fix is a change that Cozy’s managers make and a course for front-desk staff.

Signs and Cozy in 60 seconds

FOR MEMBERS

A sign marks where Focus starts, colored tape marks the edges of each zone, and labels show how to use the fridge and the shelves. Cozy in 60 seconds is a guide of eight short screens in the Cozy app, which shows a new member the zones, where to take a call and how the kitchen works.

Wi-Fi Down card

FOR FRONT-DESK STAFF AND OPERATIONS

The Wi-Fi Down card is a one-page checklist that staff follow step by step during an outage. It tells them to inform members within 10 minutes, send an update every 15 minutes and give everyone the same compensation. The head of operations makes the switch to the backup line automatic.

Log changes and Hear Them Out

FOR MANAGERS AND FRONT-DESK STAFF

Cozy’s managers make the follow-up date and the owner required in the log, add a reminder on that date and a check of open cases at each shift change, and confirm which options staff may offer. Hear Them Out teaches staff to handle the call and to follow the case through in the log.

THE COURSE: HEAR THEM OUT

The learner takes a complaint call from a member, and each choice changes how she reacts and how the call ends.

Jane, the personaA made-up but typical learner, based on the shift interviews and the issue log
  • Three years at Cozy, and knows most members by name
  • Studies in the quiet hour after lunch and is often interrupted
  • Uses the front-desk computer or her phone
→
SO THE COURSE
  • Each part takes 15 minutes or less, about 20 minutes in all
  • Progress is saved after every step
  • It works on a computer and on a phone

To decide what to teach, I used action mapping, Cathy Moore’s method for planning training. It starts from the goal, lists what staff must do on a complaint call and after it, and gives each action a practice and the information it needs. The call follows HEARD, a five-step way of handling complaints taught by the Disney Institute, with one decision for each step.

  1. HHearLet her tell it, and let her finish
  2. EEmpathizeName the feeling and say her need back
  3. AApologizeSay what Cozy got wrong, and keep names private
  4. RResolveAgree on one next step, with an owner and a day
  5. DDiagnoseKeep the case open until she says the fix worked

Sofia calls because yesterday another member took a half-hour phone call next to her in Focus. She had reported calls in Focus two weeks before, and nobody had followed up. Before answering, the learner starts a note of the call. During the call, Son, a guide character, describes Sofia’s mood after each reply, because on the phone you cannot see her face.

  1. The call. Five decisions, one for each step of HEARD
IF A DECISION WENT WRONG
  1. A week later, Sofia scores Cozy in the member survey
  2. A debrief marks each decision, and the learner rewinds to the first one that went wrong, keeping the right choices
IF ALL FIVE WENT RIGHT
  1. Part 2. The learner logs the case from the note in Cozy Desk, a practice copy of the front-desk software
  2. The course moves ahead: the case is handed over at shift change, the learner talks to the other member and calls Sofia back on the promised day
  3. Sofia scores Cozy 5 out of 5
  1. Practice. A new call, a private talk with the other member and a log entry to write
Why a call first

A realistic case lets learners see their own habits in how the member reacts.

Why survey scores as endings

Each ending ties the call to the course goal and shows which part of the call went wrong.

Why Son never hints first

Each first choice stays the learner’s, so the debrief shows the habits they bring to a real call.

Three job aids, cards that staff keep at hand at work, open during the course: the HEARD card and the Options card after the first ending, and the Log card in Part 2.
After the course, three practice activities are waiting: a new call, the private talk with the member who made the call, and a log entry to write. In the new call an AI plays the member, and an AI checks the call and the log entry. The practice activities do not count toward passing, because AI scoring can vary.

HOW IT WAS BUILT AND TESTED

I built the course as a web app and checked it with automated tests and two reviews.

I wrote the case study, the design document, which sets out how the course works and why, and the storyboard, which holds every line and screen. I designed the screens and the Cozy brand. In the app, React draws the screens, XState runs the course’s logic and Zod checks the content, and Vercel hosts it.

  • Every line of the course is checked against the storyboard, word for word
  • Browser tests go through the whole course on a desktop, a laptop and a phone
  • Buttons are large enough to tap, and the layout fits a phone 320 pixels wide
  • A test checks the contrast of every color pair
  • An expert review of the built course, from the points of view of a strict reviewer of user experience, Jane and a new hire
  • A cognitive walkthrough of the storyboard as Jane and as a new hire, which asks at each moment whether the learner would know what to do next
Next step

Test the course with real front-desk staff on their own devices during a shift, and watch where they hesitate.

Not-Home Delivery Stop

This project started from a problem at a parcel company: at stops where nobody answers the door, fewer than half end with the parcel delivered on the first attempt. I mapped what drivers need to do at those stops and what keeps them from doing it, and chose a fix for each barrier. One of the fixes is Not-Home Delivery Stop, a short online course, or module, of seven slides that I designed and built for drivers. The parcel company and its numbers are fictional and were created for this portfolio.

Try the module ↗

THE PROBLEM

At stops where nobody answers the door, 45% end with the parcel delivered on the first attempt.

When nobody answers, the driver has to decide where the parcel goes. The choice depends on the handling code on the parcel’s label, the stop note with the customer’s delivery instructions, and what the customer says when the driver calls.

These stops are called not-home stops. A parcel that goes back to the depot needs another delivery trip, and a parcel left where the rules do not allow can go missing and lead to a claim from the customer.

THE GOAL

Within three months of rollout, first-attempt success at not-home stops should rise from 45% to 65%.

At the same time, the share of those stops that lead to a claim for a missing parcel should stay at 0.5% or less. Inside the module, a driver passes with 80 out of 100 points if no stop on the last slide ends with a broken rule.

FINDING THE CAUSES

The action map found four actions and seven barriers. Training can remove five of the barriers, and two need changes in operations.

I used action mapping, Cathy Moore’s method for planning training. An action map starts from the business goal and lists what people need to do to reach it. For each action, it names the barriers that keep people from doing it, and asks whether training can remove each one or the way the work is set up should change.

7 BARRIERSTrainingOperations

Follow the steps in order

MOTIVATION + KNOWLEDGE
WHAT GETS IN THE WAY

Drivers need to follow the six steps of a not-home stop in order, including the call, before choosing where the parcel goes. Some skip the call to protect their stop rate, a number on the scorecard that tracks their work, and some are unsure of the order.

THE FIX

Slide 1 practices the order, and a job aid, the Sequence card, lists the steps. The scorecard also changes to count handovers to a neighbor or a pickup point as successes.

Choose an allowed option

KNOWLEDGE + ENVIRONMENT
WHAT GETS IN THE WAY

Drivers need to choose a place that the handling code and the customer’s instructions allow. They are unsure what SIG and AGE parcels allow, and stop notes are hard to find.

THE FIX

Slides 3 and 7 practice the rules, and the Handling codes card says what each code allows. The screen drivers see when they arrive at a stop also changes to show the code and the stop notes.

Agree on an option by phone

SKILL
WHAT GETS IN THE WAY

Drivers need to call the customer and agree on an allowed option within two minutes. Some agree to requests the rules do not allow, and their calls drift away from the point.

THE FIX

Slides 4 and 5 are practice calls, and the Call moves card lists the three things to do on a call: say who you are and what is happening, explain the allowed options, and confirm the plan and the proof of delivery.

Leave the parcel in a safe place

SKILL + KNOWLEDGE
WHAT GETS IN THE WAY

Drivers need to leave the parcel in a spot that meets four safe-place rules and take a photo that shows the house number. They misjudge whether a spot is hidden, and their photos miss the house number.

THE FIX

Slide 6 and the first stop on slide 7 ask the driver to find the safe place, and the Safe place card lists the rules: hidden from the street, protected from rain, inside the property, and doors and paths clear.

THE FIXES

The module and four job aids take the five training barriers, and operations takes the other two.

Each fix goes to the people who can act on it: drivers practice in the module and keep the job aids at hand, and operations changes two things in the work itself.

The module

FOR DRIVERS

The module has seven slides, with one question per screen. Six of them practice the four actions, one opens the job aids, and a results screen shows the score and the slides to try again.

Four job aids

FOR DRIVERS

The Sequence, Handling codes, Safe place and Call moves cards hold the steps, what each code allows, the safe-place rules and the call moves. Drivers open them on slide 2 and can open them again from a Job aids button on slides 3 to 7.

Two changes in operations

FOR OPERATIONS

The screen drivers see when they arrive at a stop shows the handling code and the stop notes. The scorecard counts handovers to a neighbor or a pickup point as first-attempt successes, so calling the customer does not count against a driver.

THE MODULE: NOT-HOME DELIVERY STOP

Seven short slides practice the four actions, one question at a time, on a phone between stops.

Drivers use the module outdoors, on a phone, between stops. So each screen asks one question and has one main button, the text is large with strong contrast, and every status, such as right or wrong, shows an icon and a word as well as a color.

On slides 1 and 3, an item can be dragged, moved with buttons, tapped into place or moved with the keyboard. After the check, each item in the wrong place says where it belongs and what goes wrong there.
Slide 4 is a call with Ms. Rivera, whose parcel needs a signature. It has recorded voices and captions, the sound plays when the driver presses Play, and two of the lines end the call early.
On slide 5, the driver types the call to Mr. Hayes, a customer played by an AI, and a second AI checks the call against the three call moves. If the AI does not work, the slide switches to a self-check.
Slide 7 is a branching scenario with two stops, in which each choice changes what happens next. The results screen then explains the rule behind each choice, and Try again reopens the slides that lost points.
The module also comes as a SCORM 1.2 package, a standard format that lets a learning management system (LMS), the software companies use for staff training, run the module and save each driver’s progress and best score.
Why one question per screen

Drivers read on a phone, outdoors, between stops, so each screen holds one thing to decide.

Why four ways to move an item

Some drivers cannot drag, so buttons, taps and the keyboard do the same job.

Why slide 5 has no points

AI scoring can vary, so the typed call is practice and the scored slides use fixed answers.

HOW IT WAS BUILT AND TESTED

I built the module as a web app and checked it with automated tests.

React draws the screens, XState runs the logic of each slide, and Zod checks the content files, where the slides, the job aids and the action map are written. The voices on slide 4 are synthetic speech, with captions timed to the audio. The module is hosted on Vercel and is also built as a SCORM package.

  • Unit tests check the logic of every slide, the scoring and the content
  • Browser tests try every slide with the keyboard alone, and the drag-and-drop slides with a mouse and with touch
  • One browser test goes through the whole module to a score of 100
  • axe-core, an accessibility checker, tests every slide at four screen widths and in high-contrast modes
  • The SCORM package is tested in scorm-again, a stand-in for a real LMS
Next step

Test the module with real drivers on their routes, since all of the testing so far is automated.
